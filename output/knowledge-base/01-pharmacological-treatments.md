## Approved Therapy: Tolvaptan

### What It Is

Tolvaptan (brand names Jynarque in the US, Jinarc in Europe) is currently the **only approved disease-modifying therapy** for ADPKD. It was approved by the FDA in 2018 and the EMA in 2015 for adults with rapidly progressing ADPKD. All other treatments for ADPKD either manage symptoms (such as blood pressure medications) or are still experimental.

Tolvaptan is a **vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist**. To understand what that means: in ADPKD, a hormone called vasopressin (also known as antidiuretic hormone, or ADH) binds to V2 receptors on kidney cells and drives the growth of cysts. Tolvaptan blocks this receptor, reducing the signal that tells cysts to grow and fill with fluid.

### The Evidence

**TEMPO 3:4 (2012)** -- The landmark clinical trial that established tolvaptan's benefit. This Phase 3 study enrolled 1,445 ADPKD patients aged 18-50 with total kidney volume (TKV) of 750 mL or greater. Over three years, patients on tolvaptan had a 49% slower rate of kidney growth compared to placebo. They also had a slower decline in kidney function (eGFR, the standard measure of how well the kidneys filter blood). Published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, this trial changed the treatment landscape for ADPKD.

**REPRISE (2017)** -- Extended the evidence to a broader population, including patients aged 18-55 with more advanced kidney disease (CKD stages 2-4, meaning eGFR 25-65). Over one year, tolvaptan slowed the decline in eGFR by 1.27 mL/min/1.73m2 per year compared to placebo. This confirmed benefit in later-stage disease.

**Long-term data** -- Follow-up studies from the TEMPO 3:4 open-label extension (TEMPO 4:4) and subsequent analyses have shown sustained benefit over periods exceeding five years. Patients who started tolvaptan earlier and continued treatment showed a consistently slower trajectory of kidney function decline compared to those who started later.

**Korean ESSENTIAL registry** -- Real-world data from a Korean patient registry has confirmed that the benefits observed in the controlled clinical trials translate to everyday clinical practice. Registry participants showed rates of kidney growth and function decline consistent with the pivotal trial results, providing reassurance that tolvaptan works outside the carefully controlled setting of a clinical trial.

**Benefit in older patients (age 56-65)** -- Pooled analyses of data from the TEMPO and REPRISE trials have demonstrated that tolvaptan provides meaningful benefit even in patients aged 56-65. This is significant because the original trials primarily enrolled younger patients, and there had been uncertainty about whether slowing kidney growth in older patients still translated to preserved kidney function. The data suggest it does.

**Adolescents** -- Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data -- meaning studies of how the drug is absorbed, distributed, and acts in the body -- have been collected in adolescent ADPKD patients. These data support appropriate dosing in younger patients and are part of the effort to understand whether earlier treatment could provide even greater long-term benefit.

**Discontinuation data** -- Studies examining what happens when patients stop taking tolvaptan have shown a **loss of treatment benefit** after discontinuation. Kidney growth accelerates back toward the untreated rate. This underscores that tolvaptan does not cure ADPKD or permanently alter the disease course -- it slows progression for as long as it is taken.

**A question worth watching, not acting on (added August 2026):** A perspective paper (not yet peer-reviewed) raised a methodological question about TEMPO 3:4, the original trial that established tolvaptan's benefit: could tolvaptan itself change how much creatinine (the substance used to estimate kidney function on a routine blood test) the body produces, in a way that makes the drug's benefit look bigger than it really is on that particular test? The authors propose re-checking TEMPO 3:4 using a different kidney-function marker (cystatin C) that isn't affected by this issue -- but that re-analysis has not been done. **This is a hypothesis, not a finding** -- no new data have been presented, and it does not change tolvaptan's overall evidence base, which rests on multiple trials and endpoints, not on one measurement method. It is listed here because a similar discrepancy showed up independently in the LIPS lanreotide trial this cycle (see Somatostatin Analogues, below) and in a broader look at how kidney-function tests are calculated (see the [Genetics & Biomarkers document](03-genetics-biomarkers.md)). Worth watching if a formal re-analysis happens; not yet a reason for any patient or clinician to reconsider tolvaptan.

**Real-world effectiveness (added May 2026)** -- Two real-world studies published in early 2026 refine the picture. The Marshall et al. UK propensity-weighted retrospective cohort (Am J Nephrology, Feb 2026; n=327, 119 tolvaptan-treated vs 208 untreated, mean follow-up 61.4 months) reported a chronic eGFR slope difference of +0.45 mL/min/1.73 m²/year (95% CI -0.37 to +1.18) -- the confidence interval includes zero, meaning the result did not reach statistical significance. Notably, **52.1% of tolvaptan patients discontinued within 12 months**, predominantly due to aquaretic side effects. Subgroup estimates in long-term-treated patients were larger (+0.58 to +0.78) but subject to survivor bias. The Canadian C-MAJOR registry (McFarlane et al., Can J Kidney Health Dis, Jan 2026) enrolled 470 ADPKD patients on tolvaptan across 24 centres; long-term outcomes are pending. **Interpretation:** these data do not undermine the trial evidence base for tolvaptan, but they suggest that average real-world effectiveness on eGFR slope is smaller than TEMPO 3:4 estimates, with treatment discontinuation as a likely major operational driver. This makes adherence support (HCTZ adjuvant, structured aquaretic-burden monitoring such as the PKD Daily diary) more relevant for getting trial-level benefit in clinical practice.

### Side Effects and Monitoring

Tolvaptan's mechanism of action produces predictable side effects:

- **Aquaresis** (excessive water excretion): Because tolvaptan blocks the kidney's ability to concentrate urine, patients produce large volumes of dilute urine -- often 6-12 liters per day. This causes frequent urination, thirst, and the need to drink large amounts of water. These effects are the primary reason many patients struggle to tolerate the drug or decline treatment. They are most pronounced at higher doses and during the first weeks of therapy.

- **Hepatotoxicity** (liver injury): Tolvaptan carries a boxed warning for potential liver toxicity. In clinical trials, approximately 4-5% of patients showed elevated liver enzymes. Serious liver injury is rare but has been reported. As a result, liver function blood tests are required monthly for the first 18 months of treatment and quarterly thereafter. In the US, tolvaptan is only available through a restricted distribution program (the Jynarque REMS) that mandates this monitoring.

- **Other side effects** include dry mouth, dizziness, and, less commonly, gout due to elevated uric acid levels.

### Measuring Tolerability: The TIPS Questionnaire (Added May 2026)

For the first time, there is now a validated instrument specifically designed to measure how much tolvaptan's polyuria affects patients' daily lives. The **TIPS questionnaire** (Tolvaptan Interference with Patients' life Scale), developed and validated by Bais, Meijer et al. at the University Medical Center Groningen (*Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation*, May 2026), was designed and validated in 152 ADPKD patients receiving tolvaptan.

Until now, clinicians asking "how much does tolvaptan bother you?" had no standardized way to measure the answer. The TIPS questionnaire gives that conversation a structured, reproducible format. This matters for:

- **Shared decision-making:** Quantifying burden before and after starting tolvaptan helps patients make informed choices.
- **Clinical research:** Trials of tolerability interventions (like HYDROPROTECT) now have a validated outcome measure for treatment burden.
- **Identifying discontinuation risk:** Patients with high TIPS scores may be at greater risk of stopping tolvaptan — and could benefit from dose adjustment or adjuvant therapy.

*Note:* TIPS was validated at a single centre (Groningen). External validation in other populations is needed before broad adoption. The questionnaire measures burden; it does not predict clinical outcomes such as kidney failure.

### Improving Tolerability: The HYDROPROTECT Trial

The aquaretic side effects of tolvaptan represent a significant barrier for many patients. The **HYDROPROTECT trial** is investigating whether adding **hydrochlorothiazide** (a common "water pill" or thiazide diuretic) to tolvaptan therapy can reduce the extreme urine volumes while preserving the drug's protective effect on cyst growth. Hydrochlorothiazide works on a different part of the kidney tubule and could, in theory, counteract some of the water loss caused by V2 receptor blockade. If successful, this combination could make tolvaptan tolerable for patients who currently cannot take it.

## Novel Investigational Agents

As of early 2026, the ADPKD drug development pipeline has expanded dramatically. Multiple major pharmaceutical companies have entered the field with novel mechanisms of action. None of these agents are approved yet, but several are in active clinical trials.

### VX-407 (Vertex Pharmaceuticals) -- PKD1 Corrector

**What it is:** VX-407 represents a fundamentally new approach. Rather than blocking a downstream signal (as tolvaptan does), VX-407 is a **PKD1 corrector** -- it aims to fix the underlying protein defect caused by certain PKD1 gene mutations. ADPKD is caused by mutations in the PKD1 gene (about 78% of cases) or the PKD2 gene (about 15%). VX-407 targets a subset of PKD1 mutations where the polycystin-1 protein is produced but folds incorrectly. The drug helps the protein fold properly and reach the cell surface, restoring some of its normal function.

This is conceptually similar to what Vertex achieved with its cystic fibrosis drugs (ivacaftor, lumacaftor, elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor), where correcting a misfolded CFTR protein transformed the treatment of that disease. Whether the same approach will work for polycystin-1 in ADPKD is now being tested.

**Current status:** Phase 2a -- the **AGLOW trial** (NCT07161037). This is a small, early-stage study enrolling approximately 24 patients. Because VX-407 targets specific types of PKD1 mutations, participants must have qualifying mutations identified through genetic testing. This is a precision medicine approach -- not all ADPKD patients would be candidates, but those with responsive mutations could potentially see substantial benefit.

**Why it matters:** If VX-407 works, it would be the first treatment to address the root cause of ADPKD rather than a downstream consequence. It could also pave the way for correctors targeting other mutation types.

### ABBV-CLS-628 (AbbVie)

**Current status:** Phase 2 trial (NCT06902558), enrolling approximately 240 patients. This is a substantially larger trial than VX-407's Phase 2a, suggesting AbbVie may be further along in development or pursuing a broader patient population. The specific mechanism of ABBV-CLS-628 has not been fully disclosed publicly. AbbVie's entry into the ADPKD space signals strong pharmaceutical industry confidence in the viability of new treatments.

### PYC-003 -- RNA Therapy

**Current status:** Phase 1 trial actively enrolling, confirmed via abstract at World Congress of Nephrology 2026 (WCN26-6082). PYC-003 is developed by PYC Therapeutics and is an antisense oligonucleotide — a type of molecule that can specifically silence or modify the expression of target genes. The Phase 1 trial (NCT06714006, approximately 116 patients) focuses on safety and tolerability.

**Updated April 12, 2026** — Phase 1 entry confirmed by WCN 2026 abstract. No safety or efficacy data disclosed yet. This is a genuinely novel mechanism class — RNA therapy represents a fundamentally different approach from all existing ADPKD drugs and could potentially address the disease at the root-cause level for patients with specific mutations. Early, but worth watching.

### JMKX003142 (Jemincare)

**Current status:** Phase 2 trial (NCT06800651), enrolling approximately 135 patients. Jemincare is a Chinese pharmaceutical company. Their entry into the ADPKD space reflects the growing global recognition of unmet medical need in this disease. Details of the drug's mechanism have not been widely published in English-language literature as of this writing.

### AZD1613 (AstraZeneca)

**Current status:** Phase 1 trial (NCT07228364), enrolling approximately 40 patients. AstraZeneca is one of the world's largest pharmaceutical companies, and their investment in an ADPKD program is noteworthy. Phase 1 trials primarily assess safety and dosing, so efficacy data are not expected for some time. The specific mechanism of AZD1613 has not been fully disclosed.

### GSK4771261 (GlaxoSmithKline)

**Current status:** Phase 1 trial (NCT06734234), enrolling approximately 84 patients. Like AstraZeneca's program, GSK's entry into ADPKD represents a major pharmaceutical company committing resources to a disease that until recently attracted limited industry investment. The mechanism of action has not been fully detailed in public sources as of early 2026.

## Repurposed Drugs Under Investigation

"Repurposed" or "repositioned" drugs are medications already approved for other conditions that may also have benefit in ADPKD. Because their safety profiles are already well-understood, they can often move through clinical trials more quickly and may be available sooner if proven effective.

### Metformin -- IMPEDE-PKD Trial

**What it is:** Metformin is the most widely prescribed drug for type 2 diabetes. In laboratory studies, it activates a cellular energy sensor called AMPK, which in turn inhibits pathways that drive cyst growth in ADPKD -- including the mTOR pathway and CFTR chloride channel activity (CFTR helps fluid accumulate inside cysts). Metformin is cheap, generic, widely available, and has decades of safety data.

**Current status:** The **IMPEDE-PKD trial** (NCT04939935) is a Phase 3 study led by the University of Queensland, enrolling approximately 1,174 patients. This makes it the **largest repurposed drug trial in ADPKD history**. The size of this study reflects both the promise of the preclinical data and the need for a definitive answer about whether metformin works in ADPKD.

**Why it matters:** If metformin proves effective, it would provide a dramatically more accessible and affordable alternative (or complement) to tolvaptan. It has a well-known side effect profile -- primarily gastrointestinal symptoms like nausea and diarrhea -- which, while unpleasant, are far less burdensome for most patients than tolvaptan's extreme aquaresis.

**What smaller trials have shown (pre-IMPEDE evidence):** A systematic review and meta-analysis published in *BMC Nephrology* (November 2025, Almeida et al.) pooled 4 smaller RCTs totaling 213 patients. The result was null: no significant effect on eGFR decline (p=0.17) or kidney volume growth (p=0.53). Gastrointestinal side effects were significantly more common with metformin (2.9-fold higher risk), and nearly 37% of patients required dose reduction or discontinued metformin due to tolerability issues. An Indian open-label RCT (n=52, 6 months) similarly found no significant difference in eGFR or kidney volume.

**What this means:** These smaller trials were individually underpowered and too short to be definitive for ADPKD. The null signal does not prove metformin does not work — it only means the current evidence base is insufficient. However, patients asking their doctors about taking metformin off-label to protect their kidneys should be aware that the existing human evidence does not support benefit at this time. IMPEDE-PKD is powered to deliver the definitive answer.

*Updated April 12, 2026 — pre-IMPEDE meta-analysis incorporated.*

### SGLT2 Inhibitors

**What they are:** Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors -- drugs like dapagliflozin (Farxiga), empagliflozin (Jardiance), and canagliflozin (Invokana) -- were originally developed for diabetes but have proven remarkably beneficial for kidney protection in chronic kidney disease (CKD) broadly, regardless of diabetes status. They are now standard-of-care for many forms of CKD. Whether this kidney-protective effect extends specifically to ADPKD (where kidney damage is driven by cyst growth rather than the typical CKD mechanisms) is an important open question.

**Evidence accumulating as of April 2026:**

- **Real-world dialysis data** (Chang et al., *Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Innovations, Quality & Outcomes*, March 2026): A retrospective propensity-matched study of 374 nondiabetic ADPKD patients from the TriNetX network found that SGLT2i users had a **65% lower risk of dialysis initiation** (HR 0.35, 95% CI: 0.21–0.61) compared to matched controls. Dialysis occurred in 10.2% of SGLT2i users vs. 23.0% of controls. This is the first real-world evidence using a patient-important endpoint specifically in nondiabetic ADPKD.

  *Important caveat:* Retrospective data with propensity matching does not establish causation. Patients prescribed SGLT2i off-label may have systematically differed from controls in ways not captured by the matching algorithm. This study strongly motivates the RCT (STOP-PKD) but does not replace it.

- **Japanese crossover RCT** (Uchiyama et al., *Kidney International Reports*, 2025): 27 ADPKD patients already on tolvaptan were randomized to add dapagliflozin or not in an open-label crossover design. Dapagliflozin showed additive effects on kidney hemodynamics and metabolic parameters on top of tolvaptan. Small and open-label, but supports the notion of additive benefit.

- **Mechanistic review** (Bou Antoun et al., *JASN*, April 2026): A comprehensive review identifies multiple convergent mechanisms by which SGLT2 inhibition could benefit ADPKD specifically: metabolic reprogramming of cyst cells (reducing their glucose dependence), hemodynamic effects (reduced intraglomerular pressure), anti-inflammatory pathways, and reduced tubular injury. The mechanistic rationale is strong.

**Current clinical trials:**

- **STOP-PKD** (NCT07280585): A Phase 3 trial of **dapagliflozin** in ADPKD, sponsored by the University of Cologne, enrolling approximately 420 patients. Expected completion 2030. This is the critical test.

- **EMPA-PKD** (NCT-based empagliflozin study): A smaller protocol-published study in Germany exploring empagliflozin in ADPKD.

- **Seliger/Watnick empagliflozin feasibility RCT** (*American Journal of Nephrology*, January 2026): A US-based randomized, placebo-controlled, 1-year pilot trial of empagliflozin (10 or 25 mg/day) vs. placebo in 50 non-diabetic ADPKD adults aged 18–55 with eGFR 30–90. The primary endpoint is safety and tolerability of the 25 mg dose; secondary endpoints include TKV, kidney function, aortic stiffness, copeptin, KIM-1, and ADPKD-specific quality of life. Design and baseline characteristics are now published; efficacy results are not yet available. This pilot cannot answer the disease-modification question on its own (n=50 is too small and 1 year is too short), but it is a useful safety/feasibility companion study to STOP-PKD.

**Updated April 12, 2026** — Evidence level upgraded from "low" to "moderate" based on accumulating real-world and mechanistic data. However, STOP-PKD Phase 3 is still needed before any definitive recommendation can be made.

**Updated April 19, 2026** — Three additional SGLT2i items added this week: the Seliger empagliflozin feasibility RCT design paper (above); a WCN26 conference abstract (WCN26-4862, ElSharkawy et al.) reporting TKV changes with dapagliflozin (effect size and methods not disclosed in abstract); and a WCN26 pathophysiology abstract (WCN26-3952, Takenaka et al.). None of these individually changes confidence in SGLT2i for ADPKD. STOP-PKD (n=420) remains the decisive trial.

**Updated May 3, 2026** — The first ADPKD-specific systematic review and meta-analysis on SGLT2 inhibitors has been published (Tantush et al., *Cureus*, April 2026): 8 studies, n=3,180 patients, pooled eGFR slope attenuation of approximately 1.34 mL/min/1.73 m²/year. Hemoglobin rose modestly with SGLT2i (consistent with the SGLT2i class effect). The initial eGFR dip was characterized as a class effect rather than ADPKD-specific harm. The non-diabetic ADPKD subgroup showed numerically larger benefit but did not reach statistical significance. **Important caveats:** *Cureus* is a low-prestige open-access journal; the underlying studies are mostly small, short-duration, retrospective, or mixed diabetic/non-diabetic cohorts; the pooled effect size of ~1.34 mL/min/1.73 m²/year is consistent with the well-established SGLT2i CKD class effect (~1 mL/min/1.73 m²/year), not an ADPKD-specific signal. **Interpretation:** the meta-analysis strengthens the rationale for the prospective RCTs already underway (STOP-PKD, EMPA-PKD, Seliger pilot) but does not establish disease modification. SGLT2i remain promising-but-unproven for ADPKD specifically; clinical decisions today should not rest on this synthesis alone.

**Why it matters:** SGLT2 inhibitors are already widely available and have a well-understood safety profile. If proven effective in ADPKD, they could be prescribed immediately to eligible patients. They could also complement tolvaptan rather than replace it. For patients who cannot tolerate tolvaptan, SGLT2 inhibitors represent the most promising near-term alternative candidate — though they must await RCT confirmation.

**Updated August 16, 2026 — CONFIDENCE LOWERED from moderate to LOW.**

A third ADPKD-specific synthesis has been published (Kanbay et al., *Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice*, August 12, 2026; registered in advance with PROSPERO; 6 studies, 451 ADPKD patients), and it is the most useful one so far because of *how* it did the comparison. It reported two different analyses side by side:

- **Comparing each patient to their own kidney-function trajectory before starting the drug:** kidney function decline slowed slightly, and this was statistically significant (+0.65 mL/min/1.73 m²/year).
- **Comparing patients on SGLT2 inhibitors against ADPKD patients not on them:** no significant difference (+2.12 mL/min/1.73 m²/year, with a confidence range so wide it spans both meaningful benefit and meaningful harm).
- **Kidney volume (TKV):** no significant difference either.

Why this matters more than it might look: comparing people to their own past is a weak study design. Kidney function measurements fluctuate, and patients tend to be started on a new drug at a moment when their numbers look bad — so they often look better afterwards regardless of the drug (statisticians call this "regression to the mean"). The stronger comparison is against similar patients who did not take the drug, and that comparison was null. The authors themselves conclude that "current evidence is insufficient to establish a beneficial effect." They are being appropriately careful; this is an honest report of a disappointing result, not an overstated paper.

**Why this changes our confidence level rather than just adding another caveat.** Four things now point the same way: (1) the encouraging pooled number from April's *Cureus* meta-analysis came from exactly the weak design this new analysis shows does not hold up; (2) the August 9 review already found kidney-volume effects "slight or inconsistent," and this now quantifies them as null; (3) the real-world dialysis result (HR 0.35) that did the most to justify our earlier "moderate" rating is currently under an unresolved published correction, and we cannot yet verify whether the correction affects the numbers; (4) two meta-analyses of substantially the same literature reached different conclusions from very different sample sizes (3,180 vs 451 patients), which tells us the underlying studies are too varied and too selection-dependent to support a confident claim in either direction.

**What "low confidence" means — and what it does not mean.** It means: plausible, biologically well-motivated, worth testing properly, but **not yet shown to work in ADPKD specifically**. It does **not** mean SGLT2 inhibitors have been shown to be ineffective — the controlled data are too sparse to rule out a real benefit. And it does **not** affect their well-established value in chronic kidney disease generally. If you have ADPKD and are taking an SGLT2 inhibitor for diabetes, heart failure, or another accepted indication, nothing in this week's evidence gives you a new reason for concern. What is no longer supportable is the claim that taking one off-label is an established way to protect ADPKD kidneys. No ADPKD-specific safety problem has emerged either.

A companion narrative review (Spatola et al., *International Urology and Nephrology*, August 12, 2026) makes the underlying structural point plainly: ADPKD patients were **deliberately excluded** from the large kidney trials that established SGLT2 inhibitors as standard care — specifically because of concerns about eGFR decline, cyst growth, and increasing kidney volume. The extrapolation from those trials to ADPKD has therefore never actually been tested. **STOP-PKD** (dapagliflozin, n=420) now carries essentially the whole question.

**Updated August 9, 2026** — A second, independent systematic review (Naeem et al., *Annals of Pharmacotherapy*, 11 studies: 4 preclinical, 1 RCT, 6 observational/case-based; PubMed/Embase/Scopus through May 2026) adds an important nuance rather than new proof: evidence is more consistent for *functional* benefits (blood pressure, proteinuria reduction, metabolic markers) than for *structural* benefit (slowing TKV/cyst growth, described by the authors as "slight or inconsistent"). In other words, SGLT2 inhibitors may help control blood pressure and protein in the urine even before it's clear whether they slow cyst growth itself — an important distinction for patients to understand, since disease modification (slower cyst growth, preserved kidney function long-term) is the higher bar. The diverse underlying studies could not be statistically pooled. This does not change the overall "promising but not yet proven for ADPKD specifically" assessment — STOP-PKD remains the trial that will answer the disease-modification question.

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists -- Tirzepatide

**What they are:** GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) such as semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) are medications originally developed for diabetes that have gained enormous attention for their effects on weight loss, cardiovascular risk reduction, and -- increasingly -- kidney protection. Preclinical data suggest potential anti-cystic effects through pathways related to cellular metabolism and inflammation.

**Current status:** A Phase 2 trial of **tirzepatide** (NCT06582875) is underway at the University of Colorado, enrolling approximately 126 patients. Tirzepatide is a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, meaning it activates two hormonal pathways simultaneously.

**Why it matters:** Given the broad metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1 RAs, there is biological plausibility for benefit in ADPKD. Many ADPKD patients also have metabolic risk factors (obesity, insulin resistance) that these drugs address. However, this is early-stage investigation, and results specific to ADPKD are not yet available.

**New preclinical caveat (added May 31, 2026):** An APS 2026 conference abstract (Garimella, *Physiology*, May 2026) reported that semaglutide reduced htTKV in Pkd1-mutant mice fed a **high-fat diet** but showed **no significant effect in mice on a standard chow diet**. This diet-dependent effect suggests that GLP-1RA benefit in ADPKD may be conditional on metabolic dysfunction or adiposity rather than representing a direct anti-cystic mechanism. If this pattern holds in humans, GLP-1RA therapy would likely benefit ADPKD patients who are overweight or obese more than lean patients. The tirzepatide trial (NCT06582875) enrolls patients with ADPKD and obesity, so this distinction may be already partially built into the trial design — but it is worth watching whether the trial pre-specifies a subgroup analysis by BMI or metabolic status. *This is abstract-only preclinical data; clinical implications are not yet established.*

### Pravastatin -- Definitive Negative Results (RCT, 2026)

**Background:** Statins (cholesterol-lowering drugs) had been hypothesized to slow ADPKD progression based on animal model data suggesting effects on cell proliferation and cyst growth. Earlier studies in children (the HALT-PKD pediatric arm) suggested a possible protective effect in young patients, which generated interest in testing statins in adults.

**Results:** A double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT (NCT03273413, Gitomer et al., *Kidney International*, February 2026) enrolled 150 adults aged 25–60 with early ADPKD (eGFR ≥60) and randomized them to pravastatin 40 mg or placebo for 24 months. The results were unambiguous:

- **Primary outcome (HtTKV annual growth rate):** Placebo 3.1%/yr vs. pravastatin 4.3%/yr — no statistically significant difference, with the numerical trend unfavorable for pravastatin.
- **Measured GFR change:** −2.3 vs. −1.4 mL/min/1.73m² — no significant difference.
- **Kidney blood flow:** No significant difference.
- **Conclusion:** Pravastatin did not slow the increase in kidney size, kidney blood flow decline, or kidney function decline compared to placebo.

**Updated April 12, 2026** — This is now a definitive negative result from a rigorously designed trial. The pediatric signal did not translate to adults.

**Updated April 19, 2026** — A *Kidney International* editorial by Vicente E. Torres (February 2026) reinforces this interpretation, concluding that the Gitomer et al. trial "does not support treatment with statins for the only purpose of delaying disease progression." Torres notes that cholesterol is biologically relevant to polycystin trafficking in primary cilia — so novel lipid-modulating mechanisms (e.g., PCSK9 inhibitors, other cholesterol-pathway interventions) are not ruled out — but pravastatin specifically does not slow ADPKD in adults.

**What this means for patients:** Statins should not be taken specifically to slow ADPKD progression. If you are currently taking a statin for cardiovascular risk management or cholesterol control, that is an entirely different and valid reason — continue as prescribed by your physician. But if you or a family member started a statin specifically hoping to protect ADPKD kidneys, this RCT demonstrates there is no benefit and possibly a slight trend toward harm (though the latter was not statistically significant).

## mTOR Pathway Inhibitors

### Historical Context

The mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) pathway is overactive in ADPKD kidney cysts and drives both cell growth and fluid secretion. This made mTOR inhibitors -- drugs like **rapamycin** (sirolimus) and **everolimus** -- among the earliest and most eagerly investigated targeted therapies for ADPKD.

### What Happened

Multiple clinical trials were conducted in the late 2000s and early 2010s. The results were **largely disappointing at tolerable doses**:

- A trial of everolimus (published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2010) showed a modest slowing of kidney volume growth over two years but no benefit in kidney function -- in fact, a slight worsening of eGFR was observed in the treatment group.

- Rapamycin trials showed similar patterns: some effect on kidney size but no clear benefit on function, and significant side effects.

### Why It Did Not Work Out

The problem was the therapeutic window. The doses of mTOR inhibitors needed to meaningfully suppress cyst growth in human kidneys produced unacceptable systemic side effects -- including mouth sores, infections, impaired wound healing, elevated cholesterol, and blood count abnormalities. These are the same drugs used to prevent organ transplant rejection, and their side effect burden at effective doses was too high for a chronic, slowly progressive disease.

### Current Status

mTOR inhibitors are **not recommended** for ADPKD treatment. However, the biology remains relevant -- the mTOR pathway is still considered a legitimate target. Some researchers are exploring kidney-targeted delivery methods or combination approaches that might achieve local mTOR suppression in cysts without systemic toxicity. Metformin's potential benefit in ADPKD is partly attributed to indirect mTOR inhibition via AMPK activation, potentially achieving a milder version of the same effect with far fewer side effects.

## Somatostatin Analogues

### What They Are

Somatostatin is a natural hormone that inhibits the secretion of many other hormones and growth factors. **Somatostatin analogues** -- synthetic versions that last longer in the body -- include **octreotide** (Sandostatin), **lanreotide** (Somatuline), and **pasireotide** (Signifor). These drugs reduce fluid secretion into cysts and may slow cyst cell proliferation.

### Primary Role: Polycystic Liver Disease

Somatostatin analogues have shown the most consistent benefit for **polycystic liver disease (PLD)**, which commonly accompanies ADPKD. Many ADPKD patients develop numerous liver cysts, and in some cases, the liver disease is more symptomatic and debilitating than the kidney disease (particularly in women, who tend to develop more severe liver involvement).

Clinical trials have demonstrated that octreotide and lanreotide can reduce liver volume in patients with severe PLD. The effect on kidney cyst growth has been more modest and inconsistent.

### Current Status

- **Pasireotide** is a newer-generation somatostatin analogue that binds to a broader range of somatostatin receptor subtypes. It has been studied specifically in ADPKD and PLD.

- **Octreotide LAR** (long-acting release) has the most clinical trial data for PLD reduction.

- **Lanreotide** has similarly shown efficacy for liver volume reduction.

These drugs are primarily used in clinical practice for patients with **severe symptomatic polycystic liver disease** rather than as a primary treatment for the kidney disease. Side effects include gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, nausea), gallstone formation, and -- for pasireotide in particular -- hyperglycemia (elevated blood sugar).

### New evidence (April 2026): Octreotide LAR meta-analysis — no kidney disease modification

A meta-analysis of 6 randomized controlled trials of octreotide LAR in ADPKD (Jin, Shi, Yu, *Frontiers in Endocrinology*, January 2026) has now been published.

- **GFR:** standardized mean difference −0.020 (95% CI −0.250 to 0.210, p=0.864) — no significant effect.
- **Total kidney volume:** standardized mean difference −0.001 (95% CI −0.376 to 0.374, p=0.996) — no significant effect.
- **Cystic kidney volume:** a borderline signal (RR 1.127, 95% CI 0.996–1.276, p=0.058) that did not reach conventional significance.
- **Adverse events:** no increase vs control.

**What this means:** The accumulated randomized evidence does not support octreotide LAR for **ADPKD kidney disease modification**. Patients asking about octreotide for kidney protection should be told that trial evidence does not support this use.

**Important distinction:** This meta-analysis concerns the **kidney** effect of octreotide LAR. Somatostatin analogues retain a legitimate role in **symptomatic severe polycystic liver disease**, where the drugs consistently reduce liver volume and symptom burden. Confusing these two indications is a common communication error.

*Updated April 19, 2026 — 6-RCT meta-analysis incorporated.*

### New evidence (August 2026): LIPS lanreotide RCT confirms the pattern with a second drug in the class

The **LIPS trial** (NCT02127437) -- a 3-year, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of monthly lanreotide 120mg in 144 adults with stage 2/3 ADPKD -- is now fully published (*Kidney International*, 2026). This is an important trial because it used **measured GFR** (a direct clearance test, considered more reliable than the everyday estimated-GFR blood test) as its primary endpoint, rather than just estimating kidney function from a blood test.

- **Primary result: negative.** No meaningful difference in measured-GFR decline between lanreotide and placebo over 3 years (annualized difference −0.3 mL/min/1.73m²/yr, 95% CI −3.4 to +2.8 -- a range wide enough that it cannot rule out a small effect in either direction, partly because the trial stopped enrolling early at 144 of a planned 180 patients).
- **A signal that didn't hold up:** the trial did find a positive effect when kidney function was estimated from a routine blood creatinine test -- but when the same patients' kidney function was checked with two other, more specific methods (cystatin C and a urine-based measurement), that positive signal disappeared. This is an important reminder that not all "kidney function improved" results mean the same thing, depending on how kidney function was measured (see the new discussion of this issue in the [Genetics & Biomarkers document](03-genetics-biomarkers.md)).
- **New safety signal:** low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) occurred in 11.1% of lanreotide-treated patients vs. 1.4% on placebo -- not previously a prominent concern with this drug class.

**What this means:** Lanreotide is now the **second** somatostatin analogue (after octreotide, above) to fail to show a kidney-function benefit in a randomized trial. Two different drugs in the same class, tested independently, point the same direction. This further weakens the already-thin case for using somatostatin analogues to protect kidney function in ADPKD. **It does not affect their established use for symptomatic polycystic liver disease** (see below), which is a different organ and a different question.

A related industry-sponsored trial of a newer octreotide formulation (**CAM2029**, Camurus) in polycystic **liver** disease posted results this cycle: liver volume shrank slightly more on treatment than placebo, but the result was only just statistically significant (p=0.0443) and was driven mostly by the placebo group's liver continuing to grow rather than the treatment group's liver shrinking -- plus one death recorded as a serious adverse event in the treatment arm. This is a liver-disease trial, not a kidney trial, and its marginal result should not be read as strong evidence either way.

*Updated August 2, 2026 — LIPS lanreotide RCT and CAM2029 PLD trial result incorporated.*

## Gene Therapy and RNA-Based Approaches

### The Promise

ADPKD is a genetic disease caused by mutations in PKD1 or PKD2. The most direct way to treat it would be to fix or compensate for the genetic defect itself. This is the rationale behind gene therapy and RNA-based approaches, which are still largely in preclinical or very early clinical development.

### CRISPR Gene Editing

**CRISPR-Cas9** is a technology that allows precise editing of DNA sequences. Researchers have demonstrated the ability to correct PKD1 and PKD2 mutations in cell and animal models. However, translating this to human therapy faces major challenges:

- **Delivery**: Getting CRISPR components into enough kidney cyst-lining cells to make a clinical difference is technically demanding. The kidneys contain millions of cells, and current delivery methods (typically viral vectors or lipid nanoparticles) have limited efficiency in reaching kidney tubular cells.

- **Safety**: Off-target editing -- where CRISPR cuts DNA at unintended locations -- remains a concern for any gene editing therapy, particularly one targeting a large organ.

- **Timing**: By the time ADPKD is clinically significant, millions of cysts may have already formed. Gene therapy would need to prevent new cysts from forming and ideally shrink existing ones.

As of early 2026, CRISPR approaches for ADPKD remain **preclinical** -- tested in laboratories and animal models but not yet in human patients.

**CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) — boosting the existing PKD1 copy:** A separate strategy, instead of editing DNA, is to use a catalytically inactive Cas9 fused to a transcriptional activator to **upregulate the patient's remaining functional PKD1 copy**. A May 2026 methodology paper (Chakraborty and Yu, *Molecular Biology Reports*, May 2026) reports a one-pot cloning approach that shrinks CRISPRa plasmids from 9–11 kb to ~6 kb, raises nucleofection efficiency in mouse Pkd1-mutant kidney cells from 16% to 54% (and from 10% to 31% in primary cells), and upregulates endogenous Pkd1 by ~3–4 fold. This is in-vitro work without an in-vivo cyst readout, so it is methodology infrastructure rather than a therapy candidate. It does, however, lower the technical barrier for academic groups testing additional polycystin-restoration angles, and it complements the small-molecule (Vertex VX-407), miR-17 (farabursen) and antisense (PYC-003) routes already in clinical development.

**CRISPRa in organoids — reducing cystogenesis (new June 2026 preprint):** A bioRxiv preprint (ss_288702390, June 2026) takes the CRISPRa strategy one step further: using a dCas9-transcriptional activator directed at the PKD1 promoter in Pkd1RC/RC-derived kidney organoids, the authors report ~3-fold PKD1 upregulation and a reduction in cyst lumen expansion compared to control organoids. This is still in-vitro/organoid work — no in-vivo cyst burden data have been reported — and the paper has not yet been peer-reviewed. The usual caveats apply: organoid-to-animal-to-human translation involves unsolved delivery, off-target chromatin activation, and immunogenicity challenges. The significance of this preprint is that it applies CRISPRa in a 3D disease-relevant model (organoids) rather than a 2D cell line, which is a step closer to physiological relevance. It will be important to watch whether the group publishes in-vivo follow-up data in a mouse model, which would be the next key milestone for this approach.

**mRNA replacement via kidney-targeted lipid nanoparticles (new June 2026 preprint):** A different restoration strategy is to deliver a working copy of the gene's *messenger RNA* rather than editing or activating DNA. A bioRxiv preprint (ss_lnp_pkd2_728835 / PMID 42282798, posted June 5, 2026) reports **collecting-duct-targeted lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) carrying Pkd2 mRNA**, which restored polycystin-2 and attenuated cyst expansion in a preclinical ADPKD model. The notable advance is the *delivery* angle: targeting LNPs specifically to the collecting duct addresses the long-standing problem of getting cargo into the right kidney cells. Important caveats: this is an unrefereed preprint with no indexed author list, the endpoint is a surrogate cyst-burden readout (not kidney function), and it targets **PKD2** — the milder genotype affecting ~15–20% of patients — rather than the dominant PKD1. mRNA also degrades, so any therapy would likely require repeat dosing, raising questions about durability and immunogenicity that the preprint does not resolve. Treat this as an early proof-of-concept for the delivery concept, not a candidate therapy.

### miR-17 Inhibitors — Farabursen (formerly RGLS8429) Phase 1 completes with first clinical signal (April 2026)

**What they are:** MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small RNA molecules that regulate gene expression. **miR-17** is a specific microRNA that is overexpressed in ADPKD cysts, where it promotes cyst growth by suppressing several protective genes including PKD1, PKD2, and others. Blocking miR-17 with an inhibitor could, in theory, slow cyst growth by restoring the expression of these protective genes.

**Development history:** Regulus Therapeutics developed **RGLS4326**, an anti-miR-17 oligonucleotide that showed early preclinical promise but encountered CNS toxicity signals in development. A next-generation molecule, **RGLS8429 — now named farabursen — was advanced into a Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05521191)**.

**What is new (April 2026):** The Phase 1 trial is now listed as COMPLETED on ClinicalTrials.gov. A conference abstract at the World Congress of Nephrology 2026 (WCN26-7987, *Kidney International Reports*) reports that farabursen:

- **increases urinary polycystin-1 and polycystin-2** — pharmacodynamic evidence that the drug is engaging its intended target and restoring expression of the proteins lost in ADPKD, and
- **reduces height-adjusted total kidney volume (htTKV) growth** in patients.

This is the **first disclosed clinical proof of concept for a polycystin-restoration approach** in ADPKD patients. It is also the first non-tolvaptan drug to show a clinical signal on a structural kidney endpoint (htTKV growth) in patients.

**What this does not yet tell us:**
- The information is a **conference abstract title only**. No sample size, effect magnitude, statistical precision, comparator detail, or full safety data are publicly available.
- Phase 1 trials are designed for safety and pharmacokinetics, not efficacy. Efficacy signals from Phase 1 are frequently not replicated in larger controlled studies.
- The study is company-sponsored (Regulus Therapeutics).
- The predecessor molecule's history is a reminder that RNA-based kidney therapies face specific technical and safety hurdles.

**What to watch next:** Regulus will need to register and run a Phase 2 trial with a proper placebo-controlled design, adequate sample size, and a longer observation window before farabursen can be judged clinically meaningful for ADPKD. Based on typical development timelines, this is 2–4 years away from any regulatory milestone.

**For patients:** This is a genuinely interesting pipeline event, not a new treatment. Do not change current care based on this disclosure. Monitor for Phase 2 announcements.

### RNA Therapy — PYC-003 Phase 1

The PYC-003 Phase 1 trial (mentioned in the investigational agents section above, NCT06714006) represents the most advanced antisense-oligonucleotide RNA approach currently in clinical testing for ADPKD. This is a space to watch closely. **Update (June 2026):** the registry was refreshed on June 8, 2026, extending primary completion from September 2026 to **November 2028** (~2-year extension) and expanding enrollment from 116 to 166 participants; status remains RECRUITING with no results posted. In practical terms, the first read on this approach is now a 2028-or-later event rather than late 2026.

### PKD1 "Upstream" Genetic Brake — A New Preclinical Mechanism (Added August 2026)

Researchers identified a previously unrecognized feature of the PKD1 gene itself: short stretches near the start of the gene (called **upstream open reading frames**, or uORFs) act as a built-in brake that suppresses how much Polycystin-1 protein gets made from the gene. In mouse models, removing this brake -- or blocking it with a **steric antisense oligonucleotide** (a designed RNA-like molecule) -- raised Polycystin-1 protein 2- to 4-fold and prevented cysts from forming.

**Why this is interesting:** most ADPKD is caused by having only one working copy of PKD1 (the other is mutated). If this brake can be safely released in humans, it could boost protein output from the one remaining working copy -- without needing to replace or edit any genes. This adds a genuinely new tool (alongside mRNA replacement, CRISPR activation, and RNA-silencing approaches described below) to the "restore the missing protein" strategy.

**What this doesn't tell us yet:** this is mouse and lab-dish (in vitro) work only. No human dosing, delivery, or safety data exist. As with all preclinical ADPKD findings, keep in mind that this field has a weak track record of mouse results translating into working human drugs.

A separate preprint reported a **combination RNA-silencing nanoparticle** (targeting two genes, TMEM16A and MCP-1, at once) that reduced kidney enlargement and cyst burden more effectively than silencing either gene alone in a mouse model of ADPKD -- another early-stage addition to the same broad RNA-therapy toolkit, with the same preclinical caveats. **Correction (August 9, 2026):** the PubMed full-text version of this same study (Giblin/Chung et al., USC, published August 2026) surfaced a conflict of interest not visible from the original preprint listing: three of the authors are co-founders of **Silver Spur Therapeutics, Inc.**, a company commercializing the peptide-micelle delivery platform used in this study. This does not invalidate the preclinical data, but it should be weighed when reading the results -- and this is not a second, independent confirmation of the finding; it is the same underlying study appearing under a second tracking ID, a recurring data-hygiene issue in this tracker's pipeline (also affected a collagen-targeted MRI fibrosis-imaging preprint the same week, where the senior author holds equity and patents in the company commercializing that imaging platform).

## The Emerging Landscape

### An Unprecedented Moment in ADPKD Drug Development

The period from 2024-2026 represents the **most active drug development period in ADPKD history**. Consider the contrast:

- **Before 2018:** No approved disease-modifying therapy existed. Tolvaptan was the only drug in late-stage trials. Research was dominated by academic groups with limited industry involvement.

- **2018-2023:** Tolvaptan gained approval and became the standard of care for rapidly progressing ADPKD. A handful of small trials explored repurposed drugs. Vertex began its PKD1 corrector program.

- **2024-2026:** The pipeline has exploded. At least six novel agents from major pharmaceutical companies (Vertex, AbbVie, AstraZeneca, GSK, Jemincare, and the developers of PYC-003) are in Phase 1 or Phase 2 trials. Three large-scale trials of repurposed drugs (metformin, dapagliflozin, tirzepatide) are underway. The total number of patients enrolled across active ADPKD interventional trials numbers in the thousands.

### Why Now?

Several factors converge to explain this wave:

1. **Validated surrogate endpoint:** The FDA's acceptance of total kidney volume (TKV) as a surrogate endpoint for ADPKD trials has made it faster and cheaper to test new drugs. Companies do not need to wait decades for kidney failure endpoints.

2. **Tolvaptan's limitations:** Tolvaptan's significant side effect burden and restricted availability have left clear room for improvement. The market opportunity is substantial -- most eligible patients either do not take tolvaptan or discontinue it.

3. **Biological understanding:** Decades of basic science research have identified multiple druggable targets in the cyst growth pathway, giving companies confidence that intervention is biologically feasible.

4. **Genetic testing advances:** Increasingly affordable genetic testing makes precision medicine approaches (like VX-407's mutation-specific strategy) practically viable.

5. **Success in rare kidney diseases:** Positive precedents from drug development in other rare kidney conditions have encouraged investment.

### Inflammation and Innate Immunity — A Research Theme to Watch (Added May 2026)

Three independent groups in May 2026 reported related findings pointing to **inflammation and innate immunity as modifiers of ADPKD progression**:

- **TLR2 signalling (Wei et al., *IJMS*, May 2026):** Stimulating Toll-like receptor 2 with a synthetic agonist accelerated cyst growth and renal fibrosis in Pkd1-mutant mice; the natural TLR2 ligands decorin and biglycan are elevated in human PKD kidneys.
- **Kidney resident macrophage (KRM) niche kinetics (Yashchenko et al., *Kidney360*, May 2026):** Cystic mice rely on in-situ macrophage self-proliferation rather than the normal monocyte-driven niche refilling, and **female cystic mice show a sex-specific deficit** that tracks with worse cyst burden, fibrosis, and kidney function.
- **Endocannabinoid system + IL-6 (Simankowicz et al., *IJMS*, May 2026; Betkar/Tam, *Molecular Medicine*, March 2026):** ADPKD patients have lower plasma anandamide and 2-AG and higher IL-6 than controls; preclinical data suggest progressive endocannabinoid system dysregulation and propose CB1R as a candidate metabolic-inflammatory target.

**What this is:** a research-prioritization signal. NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors, TLR2 antagonists, and CB1R-directed agents are tractable from a drug-development standpoint, and the convergence across groups is unlikely to be coincidence.

**What this is not:** a clinical signal. None of these reports identifies an intervention validated in ADPKD outcomes. The TLR2 work used an agonist (not antagonist); the macrophage work has no proposed druggable handle; the human endocannabinoid data is cross-sectional. ADPKD's preclinical-to-clinical translation history is poor enough that mechanistic convergence alone should not raise clinical confidence in any specific intervention.

**For patients:** these findings do not change anything in current care. They explain why several pharma companies and academic groups are moving inflammation-targeted candidates into ADPKD-relevant preclinical programs.

### HIF-1α–Pannexin-1–ATP Axis: A New Mechanistic Node (Added June 2026)

A study published in the *FASEB Journal* (May 2026, pm_42117913) identifies a previously uncharacterized signaling chain connecting cellular oxygen sensing to cyst growth in ADPKD:

In Pkd1-knockout mouse kidneys, hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (**HIF-1α**) accumulates within cyst-lining cells and drives upregulation of **Pannexin-1** — a membrane channel that releases ATP into the extracellular space. This ATP then acts on purinergic receptors (P2X4 and P2Y12) on neighbouring cells, sustaining cyclic-AMP and mTOR signaling that promotes cyst filling and cell proliferation. When the researchers chemically inhibited HIF-1α or genetically depleted Pannexin-1 in the mouse model, cystic burden was reduced.

**Why this is interesting:** HIF-1α is well-established as a driver of solid tumour growth and is already a drug target in cancer (e.g., belzutifan for renal cell carcinoma). Pannexin-1 inhibitors and purinergic receptor antagonists also exist as tool compounds. The finding suggests that hypoxic signaling within the compressed, cyst-distorted kidney microenvironment may create an autocrine/paracrine feed-forward loop that amplifies cyst growth.

**Why caution is essential:**
- This is entirely mouse-model data. ADPKD has a historically poor preclinical-to-clinical translation record, and HIF-1α inhibitors have a difficult systemic toxicity profile.
- The study used a Pkd1-knockout (null) model, which is more severe than typical human ADPKD caused by heterozygous mutations plus somatic second hits.
- Purinergic signaling is ubiquitous; antagonizing P2X4/P2Y12 globally would have effects far beyond the kidney.

**For patients:** This does not represent a near-term therapeutic development. It is a mechanistic insight that could motivate kidney-targeted delivery approaches or combination strategies in preclinical programs. No clinical trial is registered at time of writing.

### Metabolic Reprogramming — Anti-Polyamine (DFMO) and Mitochondrial Findings (Added June 2026)

Two June 2026 reports reinforce the idea that ADPKD cysts are sustained by altered cellular metabolism downstream of polycystin loss — and that this metabolism might be drug-targetable.

- **DFMO / anti-polyamine therapy (pm_42282605, bioRxiv preprint, June 5):** Five months of oral **DFMO (eflornithine)** — an FDA-approved inhibitor of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC1), the rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine synthesis — reduced cystic index, cyst number, and kidney-to-body-weight ratio in Pkd1RC/RC mice, with gene-expression normalization of many disease-perturbed pathways. Two features make this more interesting than the average preclinical hit: DFMO is an **already-approved drug** (lowering the repurposing barrier), and the experiment used a **slow-progression model** that better mimics human ADPKD than the fast models often used. Caveats remain substantial: it is an unrefereed preprint, the endpoints are surrogate cyst measures (not kidney function), there is no human data, and ADPKD's preclinical-to-clinical track record is poor.

- **Polycystin-1 cleavage and mitochondria (pm_42275149, *Am J Physiol Renal Physiol*):** In a mouse model where polycystin-1's GPS self-cleavage is disrupted, kidney mitochondria showed reduced energy production and calcium uptake specifically through Complex I (the NADH-fed entry point of the respiratory chain) but not through an alternative substrate (succinate). Mitochondrial *quantity* was normal — the defect was *functional*. This is purely mechanistic (male mice only, no treatment tested), but it ties a specific molecular lesion to a specific bioenergetic deficit and helps explain why metabolic approaches keep surfacing as candidates.

**For patients:** Neither finding changes current care. DFMO is the more watchable of the two because it is an existing drug — but there is no ADPKD trial of it, and a positive mouse result is a long way from a human therapy.

### Note on a recent retraction (May 2026)

In May 2026, *Renal Failure* retracted a 2020 paper that had reported "miR-25-3p promotes proliferation and inhibits autophagy of renal cells in polycystic kidney mice by regulating ATG14-Beclin 1" (PMID 32340512). The retraction does not provide new mechanistic data; it removes a single preclinical mechanism claim from the cited literature. Patients are unlikely to encounter this paper directly, but it is a useful reminder that **single-paper preclinical mechanism claims should not anchor patient-facing conclusions** about ADPKD. Always look for replication, RCT data, or systematic reviews when assessing whether a drug or pathway is likely to matter clinically.

### APS 2026 Preclinical Signals — Mixed Results (Added May 2026)

The American Physiological Society 2026 meeting contributed a batch of ADPKD preclinical abstracts. Three signals are notable for different reasons:

- **Netrin-1 and sex dimorphism (ss_2aff4edc):** Netrin-1 overexpression amplified cyst growth in Pkd1-mutant female mice but not males, pointing to sex-specific cyst-promotion pathways. If replicated, this could help explain the well-documented but incompletely understood sex differences in ADPKD progression trajectories. Currently speculative — single abstract, no peer review.

- **Lithium carbonate — negative preclinical result (ss_6ab8e58c):** Lithium failed to slow cyst progression in a mouse PKD model despite initial mechanistic rationale (GSK-3β inhibition). This is a useful pruning result: **lithium should not be considered a candidate for clinical investigation in ADPKD** on current evidence. The negative result is filed in finding_004 as a contradicting paper.

- **Metformin + cinacalcet in vitro synergy (ss_41fcce1a):** The combination produced greater cyst-epithelial suppression than either drug alone in tubular organoids, attributed to calcium-regulated mTORC1 modulation. This is early-stage hypothesis generation only — organoid-to-animal-to-human translation requires several additional steps.

- **miR-21 RiboTAC (pm_42149896):** A targeted RNA degrader designed to suppress miR-21 showed cyst-reduction and fibrosis-reduction in preclinical models. Mechanistic rationale is plausible (miR-21 is pro-fibrotic and elevated in PKD kidneys). No clinical trial registered at time of writing.

**Collective read:** These are incremental, hypothesis-generating results. None qualifies as a clinical signal. The lithium negative result is the most immediately actionable: it removes a candidate rather than adding one, which is valuable for prioritising future research resources.

### CFTR Inhibition Fails — A Pruning Event (Added May 2026)

The MANGROVE Phase 2a negative result (described in the "What did not work" section above) adds to the accumulating list of mechanistically plausible ADPKD therapies that failed to translate from animals to humans: statins, mTOR inhibitors (for kidney disease), somatostatin analogues (for kidney disease), venglustat (glycosphingolipid pathway), and now CFTR channel inhibition. This pattern is not a reason for pessimism about the current pipeline — the drugs that remain in active development use different mechanisms and in some cases address the fundamental polycystin defect rather than downstream effects. But it is a recurring reminder that ADPKD's slow progression, cellular heterogeneity, and redundant cyst-driving pathways make it a particularly difficult disease to treat with single-target interventions.

### V2R Inverse Agonism — A Mechanism That Could Decouple Anti-Cystic Benefit from Side Effects (Added May 2026)

A study published in *Kidney International* (May 2026) reported that **MQ232** — a peptide isolated from green mamba venom — acts as an inverse agonist at the vasopressin V2 receptor (the same receptor that tolvaptan blocks). In a Pkd1-mutant mouse model, MQ232 reduced cyst growth comparably to tolvaptan, but its mechanism profile involved non-cAMP signaling arms (Src, ERK, CRAF, Akt, RPS6, Stat3) rather than primarily suppressing cAMP production.

Why does this matter? Tolvaptan's main drawback is that it blocks V2 receptors so thoroughly that it prevents the kidney from concentrating urine, causing extreme water loss (aquaresis). MQ232's data suggest that the anti-cystic effect of V2R modulation is mediated by *different molecular pathways* than the aquaretic effect — meaning it may theoretically be possible to design a V2R-targeting drug that slows cyst growth without causing the same degree of polyuria.

**Important caveats:** This is a mouse study using a venom peptide. Venom peptides are not drugs — they cannot be taken as pills, face major delivery challenges, and require extensive modification before clinical testing. This is a mechanistic insight, not a near-term therapy. However, it could motivate new synthetic V2R ligand programs aimed at biased agonism or inverse agonism with a favorable tolerability profile, which would be complementary to the current HYDROPROTECT strategy of managing aquaretic burden after the fact with HCTZ.

### What to Watch For

Over the next 12-24 months (through mid-2027), several key milestones could reshape the ADPKD treatment landscape:

- **VX-407 AGLOW trial results:** Even preliminary Phase 2a data could validate the PKD1 corrector concept and generate major excitement.
- **IMPEDE-PKD interim analyses:** Any signal that metformin works could accelerate access, given the drug's existing availability.
- **STOP-PKD data:** Dapagliflozin results could immediately influence prescribing, since the drug is already approved for CKD.
- **ABBV-CLS-628 Phase 2 readout:** With 240 patients, this is the largest novel-agent trial and could provide robust efficacy signals.
- **Safety data from Phase 1 trials:** AstraZeneca and GSK programs will establish whether their new mechanisms are tolerable in humans.

## Summary for Patients

**What is available now (April 2026):**
- **Tolvaptan** is the only approved treatment that slows ADPKD progression. It works, but its side effects (extreme thirst and urination, plus the need for regular liver monitoring) are significant. Not all patients tolerate it, and not all patients are eligible.
- Blood pressure management with **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** remains critical for all ADPKD patients -- these do not slow cyst growth directly but protect kidney function and cardiovascular health.

**What is being actively tested:**
- Multiple new drugs from major companies are in clinical trials. The most advanced novel agent studies (AbbVie's ABBV-CLS-628, Vertex's VX-407) could report initial results within the next one to two years.
- Familiar, affordable drugs (metformin, dapagliflozin, tirzepatide) are being tested in large trials. If any prove effective, they could become available relatively quickly given their existing safety track records.
- The HYDROPROTECT trial may find a way to make tolvaptan easier to tolerate.

**What did not work:**
- mTOR inhibitors (rapamycin, everolimus) were extensively tested but produced too many side effects at doses needed for benefit.
- Pravastatin showed no benefit for ADPKD progression in a 2025 RCT, and an editorial in *Kidney International* (February 2026) reinforced this conclusion.
- **Octreotide LAR (somatostatin analogue)**: A 6-RCT meta-analysis (January 2026) shows no significant effect on kidney function or total kidney volume in ADPKD. The drug retains its role for severe polycystic liver disease (a different indication), but should not be used specifically for ADPKD kidney protection.
- **GLPG2737 — CFTR inhibitor (MANGROVE trial, May 2026):** A Phase 2a randomized trial (Gansevoort et al., *Nephrology, Dialysis and Transplantation*, May 2026; n=66, 52 weeks) tested whether blocking CFTR — the protein that regulates chloride flow into cysts — would slow cyst growth. GLPG2737 150 mg/day showed no significant effect on kidney volume growth (+8.2% vs placebo +9.2%, p=0.61) or kidney function decline (p=0.17). The trial was terminated for lack of efficacy. This was a mechanistically plausible approach — CFTR is the protein defective in cystic fibrosis and is expressed in cyst-lining cells of ADPKD kidneys, where it facilitates fluid accumulation. Despite strong preclinical data, the drug did not slow disease in this trial. This failure adds to the growing list of approaches that worked in animal models but not in human ADPKD, reinforcing the caution against over-interpreting preclinical results.

**What is still early-stage:**
- Gene therapy and CRISPR approaches are scientifically exciting but remain in the laboratory.
- **Farabursen (formerly RGLS8429, Regulus Therapeutics)** — a miR-17 inhibitor — completed its Phase 1 trial (NCT05521191) and in April 2026 reported the first clinical signal of polycystin restoration (urinary PC1/PC2 appearance) and reduced TKV growth in ADPKD patients. This is an abstract-level disclosure and requires Phase 2 confirmation.
- RNA-based antisense therapies (PYC-003) are in early clinical testing.
- The VX-407 PKD1 corrector is an early but high-potential concept that could eventually lead to mutation-specific treatment.

**The bottom line:** ADPKD patients today have more reasons for cautious optimism than at any previous point. The combination of an approved therapy (however imperfect), a robust pipeline of novel agents from well-resourced pharmaceutical companies, and multiple large trials of repurposed drugs creates a realistic prospect that better treatments will emerge in the coming years. Staying informed, discussing clinical trial eligibility with your nephrologist, and maintaining optimal blood pressure and hydration remain the most important steps patients can take.
