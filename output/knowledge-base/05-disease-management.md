# ADPKD Disease Management & Guidelines

> **Last updated:** August 9, 2026
>
> This is a living document. It synthesizes current clinical guidelines, active trials, and practical management considerations for autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Written for motivated patients and caregivers who want a thorough understanding of how ADPKD is managed today.

---

## 1. The KDIGO 2025 Guideline: A Landmark for ADPKD

In February 2025, Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) published the first comprehensive international clinical guideline dedicated to ADPKD. This was a turning point. Before 2025, clinicians worked from position statements, expert opinions, and regional consensus documents. Now there is a single, evidence-graded reference that covers evaluation, management, and treatment of ADPKD across the lifespan.

**What the KDIGO 2025 guideline covers:**

- **Diagnosis:** How to confirm ADPKD using ultrasound, MRI, and genetic testing, including which imaging criteria to use at different ages and in different genetic backgrounds (PKD1 vs. PKD2).
- **Risk assessment:** A structured approach to identifying patients at high risk of rapid progression, which directly informs treatment decisions (see Section 6 below on the Mayo Classification and PROPKD score).
- **Tolvaptan eligibility:** Clear guidance on which patients are most likely to benefit from tolvaptan, when to start, and how to monitor.
- **Pediatric recommendations:** Specific advice for managing ADPKD in children and adolescents, including screening, blood pressure targets, and the role of genetic testing.
- **Polycystic liver disease:** Practical recommendations for evaluating and managing liver cysts, a common comorbidity.
- **Pain, cardiovascular risk, and quality of life:** Guidance on managing the broader burden of ADPKD, not just kidney function decline.

The KDIGO 2025 guideline is now the key reference for clinical practice worldwide. If you are seeing a nephrologist for ADPKD, it is reasonable to ask whether your care plan aligns with these recommendations.

**Reference:** Published in *Kidney International*, February 2025.

### Regional Commentaries on KDIGO 2025

Four regional commentaries now translate the KDIGO 2025 guideline to local clinical contexts:

- **KDOQI US Commentary (Dahl et al., AJKD, April 2026):** broadly concurs with KDIGO recommendations on diagnosis, kidney manifestations, CKD management, and therapies. Acknowledges US-specific implementation barriers, including the REMS requirement for tolvaptan and access issues.
- **ERA Working Group Genes & Kidney commentary on intracranial aneurysms (NDT, May 2026):** the most clinically actionable regional commentary in this cycle. Endorses **risk-stratified ICA screening** based on family history of intracranial aneurysm/subarachnoid hemorrhage/sudden death, smoking, and blood pressure control — *not* universal screening of all ADPKD patients. Recommends **contrast-free MRA** as preferred imaging modality. Suggests **5–10 year rescreening intervals in high-risk individuals with a negative initial scan**. Importantly, it clarifies that **thoracic aortic aneurysm is associated with ADPKD** (family screening warranted), but **abdominal aortic aneurysm is not increased**. This helps align European centres with KDIGO 2025 and reduces ambiguity in clinic counselling.
- **CARI commentary for Australia/New Zealand (Abdy et al., Internal Medicine Journal, April 2026):** adapts KDIGO recommendations to the AU/NZ context.
- **Canadian Society of Nephrology commentary (Lanktree et al., *Canadian Journal of Kidney Health and Disease*, June 2026):** adapts KDIGO recommendations to Canadian practice, emphasizing multidisciplinary care, challenges in access to genetic testing, and the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in ADPKD. It was developed from the KDIGO evidence summaries via expert survey/consensus and did not re-run the underlying systematic reviews — so it adds implementation guidance rather than new evidence. The accumulation of four concordant regional commentaries (US, Europe, Australia/New Zealand, Canada) signals broad international uptake of KDIGO 2025.

These commentaries do not generate new primary evidence but reduce implementation friction in real-world practice.

### Aortic Dissection Risk — Population-Level Quantification (Added June 2026)

A Japanese nationwide database study (pm_41881380, ePublished March 2026, June 2026 print) provides the first large-scale population-based estimate of aortic dissection risk in ADPKD:

- **Study:** Nationwide Japanese administrative database, approximately 2.57 million individuals, comparing ADPKD patients to matched controls.
- **Finding:** ADPKD patients had a **hazard ratio of approximately 1.76** for aortic dissection compared to controls — a roughly 76% increased relative risk.

**Context:** The ERA Working Group Genes & Kidney commentary on KDIGO 2025 (see above) already establishes that thoracic aortic aneurysm is associated with ADPKD and that family screening is warranted — but without a large population-based estimate of the dissection hazard specifically. This Japanese study provides that number. The HR of 1.76 is clinically meaningful: aortic dissection carries high mortality and frequently presents as an acute emergency, so understanding the baseline risk in ADPKD informs the urgency with which vascular screening recommendations should be communicated to patients.

**Important limitations and caveats:**
- This is retrospective administrative-database data. Diagnostic coding accuracy for both ADPKD and aortic dissection in Japanese administrative databases is uncertain — aortic dissection may be undercoded (missed diagnoses) or the ADPKD group may be enriched for more severe disease captured at specialist centers.
- Comorbidity matching completeness is unknown; residual confounding from hypertension severity (which is both more common and more severe in ADPKD) could inflate the aortic dissection HR.
- External generalizability to non-Japanese populations requires separate validation. However, the ERA commentary's biological rationale (polycystin-1 is expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells, and its dysfunction may weaken the aortic wall independent of blood pressure) applies regardless of ethnicity.
- The paper was ePublished in March 2026 and arrived in a June 2026 print-issue batch — it is approximately 3 months old as of this writing; not a new scientific event but now formally cited.

**What this means in practice:** ADPKD patients with a family history of aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection, or sudden cardiovascular death should discuss vascular imaging (typically contrast-free MRA or CT of the thoracic aorta) with their nephrologist and cardiologist. Blood pressure control, which slows aortic wall stress, remains the most actionable intervention to reduce dissection risk in ADPKD.

## 2. Blood Pressure Management

High blood pressure is one of the earliest and most important complications of ADPKD. It often appears well before kidney function starts to decline, sometimes in childhood or young adulthood. Controlling blood pressure slows kidney growth and protects against cardiovascular disease, which remains a leading cause of illness and death in ADPKD.

### First-line medications

ACE inhibitors (such as lisinopril or ramipril) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs, such as losartan or valsartan) are the recommended first-line blood pressure medications for ADPKD. These drugs target the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which is overactive in ADPKD and contributes to both hypertension and cyst growth.

### Blood pressure targets

The HALT-PKD trial (completed 2014) established an important finding: in younger ADPKD patients (ages 15-49) with preserved kidney function (eGFR above 60), a strict blood pressure target of less than 110/75 mmHg slowed kidney growth compared to a standard target of 120-130/70-80 mmHg. This aggressive target is now widely adopted for younger patients with early-stage ADPKD, though it must be balanced against side effects like dizziness and fatigue.

For older patients or those with more advanced CKD, blood pressure targets are generally less aggressive (typically below 130/80 mmHg), guided by individual tolerance.

### Active trials in blood pressure management

- **HYDROPROTECT (NCT05373264):** This trial is testing whether adding hydrochlorothiazide (a common diuretic) to tolvaptan therapy improves outcomes. One practical motivation: hydrochlorothiazide may help counteract the massive urine volumes caused by tolvaptan, potentially improving tolerability while adding blood pressure control. Results are awaited with interest.

- **Pediatric hypertension study at King's College London (NCT07260071):** This study focuses on blood pressure monitoring and management specifically in children with ADPKD. Early detection and treatment of hypertension in childhood could have long-term benefits for preserving kidney function.

- **RDN-ADPKD: Renal denervation (NCT05460169):** Renal denervation is a catheter-based procedure that disrupts overactive nerves around the kidney arteries. The RDN-ADPKD trial is investigating whether this approach can improve blood pressure control in ADPKD patients, particularly those who are resistant to medications or cannot tolerate standard drug regimens. Prior data presented at TCT 2025 (October 2025) were single-arm and pre-post in design — a weaker evidence standard for assessing any intervention on blood pressure. **New June 2026:** A title-only abstract at the European Society of Hypertension congress (ESH 2026) is listed as "First Results from the Randomized RDN-ADPKD Study" (ss_50f98c9550). If the trial has enrolled a randomized control arm, this would be a significant methodological upgrade — but no data, sample size, or effect estimates are available from the abstract as of this writing. Watchlisted for full disclosure at or after the congress.

## 3. Tolvaptan Treatment Decisions

Tolvaptan (brand names Jynarque/Jinarc) is currently the only approved disease-modifying treatment for ADPKD. It works by blocking vasopressin V2 receptors in the kidney, which reduces the fluid secretion into cysts and slows their growth. It was approved based on the TEMPO 3:4 and REPRISE trials.

### When to start

Tolvaptan is not for everyone with ADPKD. It is recommended for patients who are at high risk of rapid progression. The key factors that inform this decision include:

- **Mayo Classification:** Patients classified as 1C, 1D, or 1E (see Section 6) are considered at high risk and are most likely to benefit.
- **Age:** Tolvaptan is typically considered in patients whose kidneys are still large enough and growing fast enough that slowing progression will meaningfully delay kidney failure. The KDIGO 2025 guideline provides a framework for weighing age, kidney volume, and rate of eGFR decline.
- **eGFR range:** The REPRISE trial enrolled patients with eGFR 25-65, while TEMPO 3:4 studied patients with better-preserved function. In general, tolvaptan is most clearly beneficial when started before kidney function is severely impaired, but benefit has also been shown in later-stage disease.

**Tolvaptan in older patients (ages 56-65):** A pooled analysis of data from clinical trials has shown that tolvaptan provides benefit even in this age group, which was initially underrepresented in studies. This is relevant because some clinicians had been reluctant to start tolvaptan in patients approaching 60, questioning whether there was enough time for the treatment effect to matter. The data suggest there is.

### Monitoring while on tolvaptan

- **Liver function tests (LFTs):** Tolvaptan carries a risk of serious liver injury (hepatotoxicity). Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and bilirubin must be checked monthly for the first 18 months, and every three months thereafter. If liver enzymes rise significantly, tolvaptan must be stopped.
- **Hydration:** Because tolvaptan blocks the kidney's ability to concentrate urine, patients produce large volumes of very dilute urine (often 6-10 liters per day or more). Adequate fluid intake is essential to avoid dehydration and dangerously high sodium levels.
- **Kidney function:** eGFR is monitored regularly to track the treatment effect.

### Side effects

The most common side effects are direct consequences of the drug's mechanism:

- **Polyuria:** Excessive urination, often requiring waking multiple times at night. This is the most burdensome side effect and the main reason patients discontinue treatment.
- **Polydipsia:** Intense thirst, driven by the need to replace lost fluid.
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Rare but serious liver damage. Caught early through monitoring, it is reversible upon stopping the drug.
- **Fatigue and impact on daily life:** The need for constant hydration and frequent urination can significantly affect work, sleep, and social activities.

The HYDROPROTECT trial (NCT05373264, mentioned in Section 2) is partly motivated by the hope that adding hydrochlorothiazide could reduce the extreme urine volumes caused by tolvaptan, making the treatment more tolerable without sacrificing its benefits.

### What happens if you stop tolvaptan?

Data from clinical trials and observational studies show that the benefit of tolvaptan does not persist after discontinuation. When patients stop taking the drug, their rate of kidney growth and eGFR decline returns to what it would have been without treatment. This means tolvaptan is a long-term commitment; stopping it, even after years of treatment, results in a loss of the accumulated benefit over time. This is an important consideration when weighing whether to start.

## 4. Polycystic Liver Disease

Most people with ADPKD develop liver cysts as well. In many cases these cause no symptoms and require no treatment. However, in a subset of patients, particularly women, the liver can become massively enlarged, causing pain, early satiety (feeling full quickly), shortness of breath, and reduced quality of life.

### Why women are more affected

Estrogen promotes the growth of liver cysts. Women who have had multiple pregnancies or who have used estrogen-containing oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy tend to have more severe polycystic liver disease. Avoiding unnecessary estrogen exposure is a practical consideration for women with ADPKD.

### Treatment options for severe polycystic liver disease

- **Somatostatin analogues:** Drugs like octreotide and lanreotide can modestly reduce liver volume in patients with severe polycystic liver disease. They are given by monthly injection. The effect tends to plateau, and these drugs are generally reserved for patients with significant symptoms.
- **Surgical options:** Cyst aspiration and sclerotherapy (draining a cyst and injecting a substance to collapse it), fenestration (surgically opening cysts), partial liver resection, and in extreme cases, liver transplantation.
- **Tolvaptan:** While tolvaptan primarily targets kidney cysts, some data suggest a modest benefit on liver cyst burden as well, though this is not its primary indication.

### Active trials for polycystic liver disease

- **GnRH agonist trial -- leuprorelin (NCT05478083):** This trial is testing leuprorelin (a GnRH agonist that suppresses estrogen production) in pre-menopausal women with symptomatic polycystic liver disease. The rationale is that lowering estrogen levels may slow or reverse liver cyst growth. This is a novel hormonal approach.
- **Foam sclerotherapy at Mayo Clinic (NCT04111692):** This trial is evaluating foam sclerotherapy as a technique for treating dominant liver cysts. Foam may distribute more effectively within a cyst cavity than liquid sclerotherapy agents, potentially improving treatment outcomes.

### KDIGO 2025 on polycystic liver disease

The KDIGO 2025 guideline includes recommendations on evaluating liver cyst burden, when to refer for specialist hepatology input, and the limited role of currently available drug therapies. It emphasizes that treatment decisions should be driven by symptoms and quality of life rather than liver size alone.

## 5. Pain Management

Chronic and acute pain are common in ADPKD, arising from cyst growth, cyst rupture, cyst infection, kidney stones, and compression of surrounding structures. Pain management is often inadequate because it falls between specialties, and opioid use carries significant risks in patients with kidney disease.

### KDIGO 2025 on pain

The KDIGO 2025 guideline addresses pain management, recommending a stepwise approach:

1. Identifying the cause of pain (cyst hemorrhage, infection, stone, musculoskeletal)
2. Non-pharmacological measures (heat, positioning, physical therapy)
3. Acetaminophen (paracetamol) as first-line analgesia
4. Avoidance of NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) due to kidney toxicity
5. Judicious use of opioids with awareness of addiction risk
6. Procedural interventions (cyst aspiration, sclerotherapy, surgical decompression) for refractory cases

### Somatosensory phenotyping study (NCT06969028)

Researchers at KU Leuven are conducting a study to better characterize the types of pain experienced by ADPKD patients using somatosensory phenotyping. This involves detailed testing of how patients perceive different pain stimuli (pressure, temperature, touch). The goal is to identify distinct pain mechanisms in ADPKD, which could lead to more targeted and effective pain treatments. This is a research study rather than a treatment trial, but it addresses a real gap in understanding.

### Cyst Sclerotherapy — Largest Reported Series to Date (Added August 2026)

Sclerotherapy involves draining a large, symptomatic cyst and then injecting an irritant solution (sodium tetradecyl sulfate, the agent used in this study) to scar down and collapse the cyst wall, preventing it from refilling. A retrospective review from Toronto covering 306 ADPKD patients and 567 treated cysts (2014–2022) is the largest such series published to date:

- **99% of treated cysts shrank** at first follow-up (around 5 months), with continued shrinkage in 80% of cysts at long-term follow-up (roughly 3.7 years later).
- Average total kidney volume fell by 371 cm³ -- a meaningful reduction for symptom relief.
- A companion lab study (using pig bladders as a stand-in tissue model) showed the treatment strips away the inner lining of the cyst without damaging deeper tissue layers, supporting a plausible mechanism.

**An important caution:** the same patients' kidney function (measured by eGFR) actually *declined* over the long-term follow-up period in this study, and creatinine rose. The published paper describes the treatment as "associated with reduced renal function decline," but the data made available do not include an untreated comparison group or an expected-decline benchmark to support that specific claim -- there is nothing to show the decline would have been *worse* without treatment. **Cyst sclerotherapy should be understood as a symptom-management procedure for pain and mass-effect from large individual cysts, not as a treatment that slows overall kidney function decline.** That distinction matters if you or your doctor are discussing this procedure: it is a reasonable option for cyst-related pain, but it is not a substitute for tolvaptan or other disease-modifying approaches.

## 6. Progression Assessment

Not all ADPKD progresses at the same rate. Some patients reach kidney failure in their 30s or 40s; others maintain adequate kidney function into their 70s or beyond. Accurately assessing the risk of rapid progression is essential because it determines who benefits most from disease-modifying treatment (currently tolvaptan) and how closely patients need to be monitored.

### Mayo Classification (1A-1E)

The Mayo Imaging Classification is the most widely used tool for risk stratification in ADPKD. It uses height-adjusted total kidney volume (htTKV) measured by MRI or CT and the patient's age to assign a class:

- **Class 1A:** Lowest risk. Kidney growth is slow.
- **Class 1B:** Low-to-moderate risk.
- **Class 1C:** Moderate risk. This is typically the threshold where tolvaptan treatment begins to be considered.
- **Class 1D:** High risk.
- **Class 1E:** Highest risk. Rapid kidney growth and early kidney failure are expected.

Patients in classes 1C through 1E are generally considered candidates for tolvaptan, though the decision also depends on age, current eGFR, and individual patient factors.

### PROPKD score

The PROPKD score is a complementary tool that uses clinical and genetic information to predict the risk of progression to kidney failure. It incorporates:

- Sex (male = higher risk)
- Early onset of hypertension (before age 35)
- First urological event before age 35 (such as visible blood in the urine, pain, or cyst infection)
- Mutation type (PKD1 truncating mutations carry the highest risk)

A higher PROPKD score indicates a greater likelihood of reaching kidney failure earlier in life. It is particularly useful when imaging-based classification is ambiguous or unavailable.

### When to initiate disease-modifying therapy

The decision to start tolvaptan is based on the combined picture: Mayo class, PROPKD score, rate of eGFR decline over time, family history, and patient preferences. The KDIGO 2025 guideline provides a structured approach, but the decision is ultimately individualized and should involve shared decision-making between the patient and their nephrologist.

### Monitoring intervals

- **Imaging (MRI preferred):** Typically every 1-3 years to track kidney volume. More frequent imaging may be warranted if treatment decisions are pending or if there is uncertainty about progression rate.
- **eGFR:** At least annually, more frequently (every 3-6 months) in patients with declining function or those on tolvaptan.
- **Blood pressure:** Regularly at clinic visits, and ideally with home monitoring.
- **Urinalysis:** Periodic checks for proteinuria and hematuria.

### Kidney Stones: A Possible Additional Risk Marker (Added August 2026)

A prospective Korean cohort study (410 patients, followed a median of 5.2 years) found that ADPKD patients with **CT-confirmed kidney stones** (nephrolithiasis) -- carefully distinguished from calcified cyst walls, which can look similar on imaging -- had a substantially higher chance of eventually needing dialysis or transplant (renal replacement therapy) than those without stones (adjusted hazard ratio 2.84).

**What this could mean:** if confirmed by further research, kidney stones could become an additional, practical marker -- alongside Mayo Class and genetic information -- to help identify patients who need closer monitoring.

**What this does NOT yet mean:** this is a single observational study. It cannot tell us whether stones actually *cause* faster kidney decline, or whether people who are prone to stones simply tend to have more severe underlying disease for other reasons. It also does not show that treating or preventing kidney stones changes a patient's kidney trajectory. Only 51 patients in the study actually reached the dialysis endpoint, which makes the estimate imprecise. **Do not read this as a reason to pursue aggressive stone prevention specifically to protect kidney function** -- that connection has not been established. Routine stone management (hydration, dietary guidance, urological care when stones cause symptoms) remains appropriate regardless.

## 7. Transplant Considerations

Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for kidney failure in ADPKD. Outcomes after transplant in ADPKD are generally good -- often better than for many other causes of kidney failure, because ADPKD patients tend to be otherwise relatively healthy.

### Timing

- Transplant planning should begin when eGFR falls below about 20 mL/min, or earlier if decline is rapid.
- Pre-emptive transplantation (before dialysis becomes necessary) is associated with better outcomes and is encouraged when a living donor is available.
- Referral to a transplant center is appropriate when eGFR reaches approximately 20-25 mL/min.

### Outcomes

- Graft survival rates in ADPKD recipients are comparable to or better than the general transplant population.
- ADPKD patients tend to have fewer comorbidities (such as diabetes) than many other kidney failure patients, which contributes to good surgical outcomes.
- Long-term immunosuppression carries its own risks (infection, cancer, cardiovascular disease), which apply equally to ADPKD recipients.

**Updated May 2026 — large registry data:** The ANZDATA Registry analysis (Jefferis et al., *BMC Nephrology*, Sep 2025; n=63,875 patients commencing kidney replacement therapy in Australia/New Zealand 1963–2020, including 2,466 with ADPKD) provides the largest published comparison of KRT outcomes for ADPKD vs. other kidney failure causes:

- **Lower all-cause mortality on dialysis** (adjusted hazard ratio 0.71, 95% CI 0.67–0.75) compared to other KRT patients.
- **Similar post-transplant survival** (aHR 0.96 — not significantly different).
- **Slightly better kidney allograft survival** (aHR 0.87).
- **Cardiovascular death rates similar** — 27.5% in ADPKD vs. 27.6% in non-ADPKD KRT patients.

**Why is dialysis mortality lower in ADPKD?** This likely reflects a case-mix advantage: ADPKD patients who reach kidney failure tend to be younger and to have fewer other health conditions (particularly diabetes and cardiovascular disease) compared to patients with kidney failure from other causes. This survival advantage does not mean ADPKD is a "milder" disease — it simply means that by the time patients reach dialysis, their overall health is often better than average.

**Important:** The similar cardiovascular death *proportion* despite lower overall mortality is a reminder that ADPKD carries real cardiovascular risks (longstanding hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, intracranial aneurysms, aortic dilation) that do not disappear on dialysis. These risks need continued management after starting KRT.

### Dialysis modality: peritoneal dialysis vs. hemodialysis (Added August 2026)

A separate, much larger US registry study (USRDS, n=53,651 ADPKD patients reaching end-stage kidney disease, 2000–2021) looked specifically at the choice *between* peritoneal dialysis (PD, a home-based dialysis method using the abdominal lining as a filter) and hemodialysis (HD, typically done at a dialysis center). Some clinicians are hesitant to recommend PD to ADPKD patients out of concern that enlarged, cyst-filled kidneys might cause mechanical complications with the PD catheter or fluid exchanges.

- 26% of the ADPKD patients in this cohort used PD at some point.
- After statistically adjusting for age, sex, health conditions, and other factors, PD use was associated with **lower mortality** and a **higher chance of eventually receiving a kidney transplant** compared to HD, and this association grew stronger the longer patients stayed on PD.

**How to read this:** this is reassuring — it suggests ADPKD patients who are reasonable PD candidates are not put at a disadvantage by choosing PD, and may do at least as well as those on HD. But this is registry data, not a randomized trial: patients who end up on PD tend to be younger, healthier, and more able to manage self-care dialysis in the first place, and statistical adjustment cannot fully rule out that this "healthier patient" selection — rather than PD itself — explains some or all of the better outcomes. **This is useful reassurance for the counseling conversation about dialysis-modality choice, not proof that switching a specific patient from HD to PD would improve their individual outcome.**

### Living donor considerations

- Family members who are potential donors must be carefully evaluated for ADPKD themselves, given the 50% inheritance risk.
- Genetic testing and imaging of potential donors are essential. A negative genetic test in a first-degree relative older than 30 with normal kidney imaging is generally reassuring.
- Unrelated living donors (spouses, friends) avoid the concern about inherited ADPKD entirely.

### Native nephrectomy

Many ADPKD patients have massively enlarged kidneys at the time of transplant. Whether to remove one or both native kidneys (nephrectomy) is a case-by-case decision:

- **Before transplant:** Sometimes performed to create space in the abdomen for the new kidney, or to address recurrent infections or pain.
- **At the time of transplant:** Simultaneous nephrectomy adds surgical complexity and is generally avoided unless necessary.
- **After transplant:** May be needed if the native kidneys continue to cause problems (pain, infection, bleeding).

The trend is toward conservative management (leaving native kidneys in place) unless there is a clear indication for removal.

**Updated May 2026 — perioperative complication data:** A retrospective single-centre study (Helal Birjandi et al., International Urology and Nephrology, April 2026, n=124) reported a **58.9% overall and 25% major perioperative complication rate** in ADPKD native nephrectomy. Independent risk factors for complications included **lower preoperative hemoglobin** and **right-sided nephrectomy**. **Simultaneous bilateral nephrectomy carried the highest complication burden.** Minimally invasive surgery did not independently reduce complications once specimen size was accounted for. This single-centre series reinforces the case for **selective use of native nephrectomy** rather than routine performance, particularly with caution around simultaneous bilateral procedures and patients with low preoperative hemoglobin.

## 8. Pediatric ADPKD

ADPKD is not solely an adult disease. Kidney cysts can be detected in children, and complications such as hypertension can begin in childhood. Managing ADPKD in children involves balancing the benefits of early detection and intervention against the psychological and practical burdens of a chronic disease diagnosis during development.

### Screening children of affected parents

Each child of an ADPKD parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the condition. Whether and when to screen is a nuanced decision:

- **Routine ultrasound screening** of at-risk children is generally recommended, primarily to monitor for hypertension and kidney complications that benefit from early treatment.
- **The KDIGO 2025 guideline** supports evaluating children of affected parents, emphasizing blood pressure monitoring as a priority.
- Some families choose not to screen children until adulthood, particularly if there is no clinical concern. This is a valid choice, and no single approach is universally correct.

### Active pediatric trials

- **Tolvaptan in children (NCT04782258):** Clinical trials are underway to evaluate the safety and efficacy of tolvaptan in pediatric ADPKD patients. If effective and well-tolerated, this could open the door to earlier disease-modifying treatment, potentially preserving kidney function over a longer lifespan.

- **Pediatric hypertension monitoring (NCT07260071):** The King's College London study focuses specifically on detecting and managing high blood pressure in children with ADPKD. Early blood pressure control may be one of the most impactful interventions available for young patients.

- **ARPKD registry at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (NCT01401998):** While this registry focuses on autosomal recessive PKD (ARPKD), a genetically distinct and typically more severe childhood kidney disease, it provides important infrastructure for understanding early-onset polycystic kidney disease broadly. ARPKD data sometimes informs approaches to severe early-onset ADPKD as well.

- **CHOP ADPKD study (NCT04338048):** This study at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia is focused specifically on ADPKD in children, contributing to the growing understanding of how the disease behaves and can be managed in younger patients.

### Blood pressure and cardiac remodelling in children — new data (Added May 2026)

A single-centre cross-sectional study (Heming et al., *Pediatric Nephrology*, May 2026, n=116 children <18 years with ADPKD) adds important data on the frequency and consequences of hypertension in pediatric ADPKD:

- **12% overt hypertension** (n=14) on standard clinic blood pressure measurement.
- **7% masked hypertension** (n=8) detected only on ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) — not visible on standard clinic measurement.
- **Combined elevated-BP prevalence: 19%** — approximately 1 in 5 children had some form of elevated BP.
- Children with elevated BP had **higher cyst burden** (56% vs 34%, p=0.036), **more left ventricular hypertrophy** (LVH: 16.6% vs 4.6%, p=0.034), and **greater relative LV wall thickness** (0.34 vs 0.29, p<0.001) compared to normotensive children.

**What this means in plain language:** about 1 in 5 children with ADPKD has elevated blood pressure, but nearly 40% of those cases are "masked" — they only show up on 24-hour blood pressure monitoring, not during a standard clinic visit. Children with elevated blood pressure have more cysts and early signs of heart stress (LVH). This is a strong argument for routine ABPM in children with ADPKD, not just spot checks at clinic visits.

**Limits of this evidence:** single-centre, cross-sectional design — the study cannot establish whether elevated BP drives more cysts, or whether more cysts drive elevated BP. The tertiary referral bias likely means hypertension prevalence is higher in this cohort than in the general pediatric ADPKD population. n=116 is modest. This evidence supports surveillance practice changes but is not sufficient for guideline-level recommendations on its own.

### Macroscopic hematuria in childhood — what does it mean? (Added May 2026)

The largest pediatric ADPKD macroscopic-hematuria series to date (Seeman et al., *European Journal of Pediatrics*, May 2026, n=289 across four European tertiary centres) gives nephrologists a numeric anchor for counselling families after a hematuria episode in a child with ADPKD:

- **7.2% (21/289)** of children with ADPKD experienced macroscopic hematuria. It was the **second most frequent presenting symptom** in the cohort.
- Common causes: **ruptured cyst, urinary tract infection, urolithiasis**.
- Children with macroscopic hematuria more often had:
  - **Higher antihypertensive use:** 14.3% vs 3.1% (p=0.017)
  - **More elevated blood pressure** (≥75th percentile): 95% vs 64% (p=0.002)
  - **More cysts on imaging:** median 20 vs 10 (p=0.038)

**What this means in plain language:** if a child with ADPKD has a macroscopic hematuria episode, the prior probability of more advanced cyst burden and elevated blood pressure is meaningfully higher than in a child without hematuria. This supports closer blood pressure and imaging follow-up after such an episode, even if the immediate cause is benign (e.g., cyst rupture).

**Limits of this evidence:** the design is retrospective; the four European centres are tertiary referral sites, so the cohort likely over-represents more advanced pediatric ADPKD; only 21 children had macroscopic hematuria, so subgroup statistics are sensitive to a few cases; the design cannot say whether elevated blood pressure precedes or follows hematuria. This is **hypothesis-generating evidence with practical counselling value**, not a guideline change.

### Very Early-Onset ADPKD: How Different Is It? (Added August 2026)

A meta-analysis pooling 19 studies (736 children in total: 335 with very-early-onset ADPKD, or "VEO-ADPKD," and 401 with typical-onset pediatric ADPKD) put numbers on a pattern clinicians have long recognized: children diagnosed very early (often before or shortly after birth) tend to have a meaningfully more severe disease course than children diagnosed later in childhood.

- Children with VEO-ADPKD had higher odds of **hypertension, protein in the urine, chronic kidney disease, and kidney failure** than children with typical-onset pediatric ADPKD.
- **Biallelic PKD1 mutations** (inheriting an altered PKD1 copy from *both* parents, which is rare) were found **only** in the VEO group, not in the typical-onset group at all -- consistent with a "the more affected copies, the more severe the disease" pattern. Children with this rare biallelic pattern tend to have a course that more closely resembles the more severe, recessively-inherited form of PKD (ARPKD) than typical single-gene ADPKD.
- Sadly, cases of death before or shortly after birth were also seen only in the VEO group.

**Genetic testing implication:** for a fetus or infant presenting with very severe, very-early cystic kidney disease, this supports pursuing genetic testing early to check specifically for biallelic PKD1 involvement, since it carries different prognostic and counselling implications than typical ADPKD (see the genetics document, [03-genetics-biomarkers.md](03-genetics-biomarkers.md), for how modern sequencing catches variants that older testing methods might miss).

**Limits of this evidence:** every one of the statistical ranges reported in this meta-analysis just barely excluded "no difference at all" -- meaning the true size of the risk difference is genuinely uncertain, even though the direction (VEO is worse) is consistent across studies. The underlying studies are small, retrospective, and were not specifically designed to test this question, so this is best read as **confirming and quantifying** an existing clinical impression rather than a new discovery.

### Ethical considerations of early genetic testing

Genetic testing can definitively confirm or exclude ADPKD in a child. However, it raises important ethical questions:

- A positive result means diagnosing a child with a lifelong condition that may not cause problems for decades. This can affect self-image, family dynamics, and insurability (depending on jurisdiction).
- A negative result provides reassurance but requires high-quality testing to be reliable.
- Many genetics guidelines recommend deferring predictive genetic testing for adult-onset conditions until the child is old enough to participate in the decision (typically mid-to-late adolescence), unless there is a medical reason to test earlier (such as blood pressure abnormalities or visible cysts on imaging).
- The KDIGO 2025 guideline acknowledges these considerations and supports family-centered decision-making.

## 9. Pregnancy in ADPKD

Pregnancy is an important consideration for people of childbearing age with ADPKD. For many years, clinical concern about whether pregnancy might accelerate kidney disease was based largely on case reports and small observational studies that suggested increased proteinuria and blood pressure during gestation in affected women.

### What the best available evidence now says (Updated May 2026)

A secondary analysis of the HALT-PKD trial dataset (Lapierre-Nguyen et al., *JASN*, May 2026; n=455 women) provides the most rigorous data yet on this question. The key findings:

- **Prior pregnancies were not significantly associated with ADPKD progression** — neither eGFR decline, total kidney volume change, nor a composite kidney failure outcome differed significantly between women with 1–2 or 3+ prior pregnancies versus women with no prior pregnancies, after multivariable adjustment.
- **In-study pregnancy (n=13 women who became pregnant during the trial, matched 1:4 with non-pregnant women) also showed a null result** — the eGFR slope difference was −0.14 mL/min/1.73m²/year (95% confidence interval: −2.72 to +2.44). This is a reassuring result, but the extremely small sample size means the confidence interval spans a range that includes no effect and a clinically meaningful negative effect. This analysis alone cannot rule out moderate harm.

**Plain-language summary:** Based on the best available evidence, pregnancy does not appear to substantially accelerate kidney disease progression in women with early-to-moderate ADPKD. This is reassuring for patients considering pregnancy.

### Important limitations — what this finding does NOT tell you

This evidence reassures on one specific question (kidney disease progression) but does not address several others:

1. **Obstetric outcomes:** Preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, preterm birth, and other pregnancy complications may occur at higher rates in ADPKD patients than in the general population. This study did not assess these outcomes.
2. **Advanced CKD:** HALT-PKD enrolled predominantly patients with relatively preserved kidney function (mostly eGFR >60 in Study A). The reassurance may not extend to patients with eGFR <45 or significant proteinuria.
3. **Sample size:** The in-study pregnancy analysis (n=13) is too small to provide statistical precision. Absence of evidence is not the same as evidence of absence for harm.
4. **Generalizability:** HALT-PKD participants were mostly Caucasian and non-diabetic; the findings may not apply equally to more diverse or higher-risk populations.

### Practical guidance

- Women with ADPKD who are considering pregnancy should be counselled by a **multidisciplinary team** including nephrology and obstetrics/maternal-fetal medicine.
- **Blood pressure control** before and during pregnancy is particularly important; discuss which medications are safe during pregnancy (ACE inhibitors and ARBs are **not** safe in pregnancy).
- Women with eGFR below 45 or significant proteinuria should receive additional counselling about both pregnancy risks and anticipated post-pregnancy kidney trajectory.
- Tolvaptan is generally **stopped before planned pregnancy** due to absence of safety data in human pregnancy.
- **Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)** is an option for couples who wish to avoid passing ADPKD to their children; eligibility and access vary by country (e.g., legal since 2015 in Italy for monogenic late-onset conditions).

## 10. Shared Decision Making

ADPKD management increasingly involves complex trade-offs: whether to start tolvaptan (with its significant lifestyle impact), when to pursue transplant evaluation, how aggressively to target blood pressure, and whether to screen children. These are not purely medical decisions; they depend on patient values, life circumstances, and tolerance for risk and side effects.

### Seoul National University study (NCT06618638)

A study at Seoul National University is formally evaluating shared decision-making models for ADPKD treatment choices. The goal is to develop structured tools and processes that help patients and their clinicians navigate these decisions together, ensuring that patients are fully informed and that their preferences are meaningfully incorporated into the care plan.

This is an important area of research because the quality of decision-making in chronic disease management directly affects adherence, satisfaction, and outcomes. For a disease like ADPKD, where the main available treatment (tolvaptan) requires significant lifestyle adaptation, shared decision-making is not just a nice idea -- it is a practical necessity.

### Practical tips for patients

- **Before starting tolvaptan:** Ask your nephrologist to walk you through what daily life on tolvaptan looks like -- the fluid intake, the urination frequency, the monitoring schedule. Ask about other patients' experiences.
- **Risk assessment:** Ask where you fall on the Mayo Classification and whether a PROPKD score has been calculated. Understanding your personal risk helps put treatment decisions in context.
- **Second opinions:** For major decisions (starting or stopping tolvaptan, transplant timing, native nephrectomy), a second opinion from a PKD specialist center is reasonable and often welcome.
- **Clinical trials:** Ask whether any active clinical trials are relevant to your situation. Participation contributes to advancing treatment for everyone with ADPKD.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The KDIGO 2025 guideline** (published February 2025 in *Kidney International*) is the first comprehensive international guideline for ADPKD and should be the foundation of your care plan.
2. **Blood pressure control** with ACE inhibitors or ARBs is the most well-established intervention. A target below 110/75 mmHg is appropriate for younger patients with preserved kidney function.
3. **Tolvaptan** slows progression in high-risk patients but requires commitment to lifestyle changes and regular monitoring. Its benefit does not persist after stopping.
4. **Polycystic liver disease** disproportionately affects women and is influenced by estrogen exposure. Treatment is symptom-driven.
5. **Risk stratification** using the Mayo Classification and PROPKD score guides treatment decisions. Not everyone with ADPKD needs tolvaptan.
6. **Transplant outcomes** in ADPKD are generally favorable. Planning should start early.
7. **Pediatric management** is evolving: hypertension is common (12% overt + 7% masked in cross-sectional data) and associated with LVH and higher cyst burden. Routine ambulatory BP monitoring is warranted.
8. **Pregnancy:** the best available evidence (HALT-PKD secondary analysis, n=455 women) does not show significant acceleration of ADPKD progression by prior pregnancy. The in-study pregnancy analysis (n=13) is reassuring but underpowered. Obstetric outcomes are a separate concern. Multidisciplinary counselling is essential.
9. **KRT outcomes:** ADPKD patients on dialysis have lower all-cause mortality than other KRT populations (ANZDATA n=63,875), largely reflecting a case-mix advantage. Cardiovascular death rates are similar.
10. **Shared decision making** is not optional — it is essential for navigating the real-world trade-offs of ADPKD treatment.
