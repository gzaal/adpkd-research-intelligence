## Overview

Dietary and lifestyle changes are among the most actionable areas for people living with ADPKD. While pharmacological treatment (primarily tolvaptan) remains the cornerstone of disease management, a growing body of research suggests that what you eat, how much you drink, and how you manage your weight may meaningfully influence disease progression. This document covers the current state of evidence -- what is known, what is promising but still unproven, and what is safe to try.

The strongest signal comes from metabolic interventions, particularly ketogenic diets and ketone supplementation. Other approaches -- hydration therapy, sodium restriction, protein management -- have varying levels of evidence but are generally low-risk and consistent with good kidney health.

## 1. Ketogenic Diets

**This is the most actively studied dietary intervention for ADPKD.** The core idea is that cyst cells in polycystic kidneys depend heavily on glucose for energy (a metabolic shift similar to what is seen in cancer cells). By dramatically reducing carbohydrate intake and shifting the body into ketosis -- where fat and ketone bodies become the primary fuel -- you may be able to "starve" cyst growth. Animal model studies from 2019 onwards showed striking reductions in kidney cyst burden with ketogenic diets, prompting several human trials.

### KETO-ADPKD Trial (NCT04680780)

- **What:** A randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted at the University of Cologne, Germany. Three arms: ketogenic diet, 3-day water fasting (repeated monthly), and a control group receiving standard dietary counseling. 66 participants enrolled.
- **Published:** Cell Reports Medicine, November 2023.
- **Key findings:** The trial demonstrated that both ketogenic diet and intermittent water fasting are feasible in ADPKD patients over 3 months. Importantly, participants who achieved higher levels of ketosis (measured by blood beta-hydroxybutyrate, or BHB) showed greater reductions in total kidney volume (TKV). This suggests that the *degree of ketosis* may matter more than the specific method used to achieve it.
- **Follow-up analyses:** Multiple secondary analyses have been published in Nephrology Dialysis Transplantation (NDT) during 2024 and 2025, examining safety markers, metabolic parameters, and predictors of response. These generally support the feasibility profile but underscore that longer trials are needed to confirm durable benefit on kidney volume and function.
- **Limitations:** The trial was short (3 months), relatively small, and was designed primarily to test feasibility rather than definitive efficacy. The kidney volume reductions, while encouraging, need confirmation in larger, longer studies.

### GREASE II Trial

- **What:** A Phase 2 trial evaluating the efficacy of a ketogenic diet over 24 months -- substantially longer than KETO-ADPKD. Presented at ERA (European Renal Association) congress.
- **Why it matters:** The 24-month duration addresses one of the main criticisms of KETO-ADPKD (its short timeframe). If ketogenic diets can be maintained and continue to show benefit over 2 years, the case for recommending them becomes much stronger.
- **Status as of April 2026:** Results from this trial are awaited or recently presented in abstract form. The trial design represents a meaningful step toward establishing whether ketogenic diets can be a standard dietary recommendation for ADPKD.

### Ohio State University Study (NCT06325644)

- **What:** A study of a Well-Formulated Ketogenic Diet (WFKD) in PKD patients. Recruiting approximately 20 participants.
- **Why it matters:** The "well-formulated" emphasis is important. Many people attempt ketogenic diets without proper nutritional planning, which can lead to deficiencies. This study uses a structured, medically supervised approach, which may provide a model for how ketogenic diets should be implemented in kidney disease patients.
- **Status as of April 2026:** Recruiting.

### Japanese Ketogenic Metabolic Intervention — 12-Month Interim (WCN26-7921)

- **What:** A 12-month interim analysis from a Japanese cohort undergoing ketogenic metabolic intervention in ADPKD, presented at the World Congress of Nephrology 2026 (WCN26-7921, *Kidney International Reports*, April 2026). Principal investigators include Kawano, Koizumi, Kimura, Ikehara, with Thomas Weimbs as co-author.
- **Why it might matter:** Extends ketogenic data beyond the original European KETO-ADPKD population and provides a longer follow-up duration (12 months vs the original 3 months).
- **What limits confidence:** Conference abstract only — no sample size, comparator, effect sizes, or statistical detail disclosed. Co-author Weimbs has a commercial relationship with Santa Barbara Nutrients (the Ren-Nu manufacturer), which is a relevant disclosure when interpreting any ketogenic-ADPKD signal from this group. Until the full abstract/paper appears, this should be treated as a pipeline/tracking item, not independent replication.

### Nutritional Adequacy Studies (Journal of Nephrology, 2025-2026)

A practical concern with ketogenic diets in ADPKD is that kidney disease patients already face dietary restrictions (limits on sodium, potassium, phosphorus, and sometimes protein). Layering a ketogenic diet on top of these restrictions creates a complex nutritional puzzle. Recent studies published in the Journal of Nephrology (2025-2026) have examined whether it is possible to maintain adequate nutrition while following a ketogenic diet in ADPKD patients, particularly those with reduced kidney function. Early findings suggest it can be done safely with careful planning and professional guidance, but it requires close monitoring of electrolytes and micronutrients.

### Nutritional Adequacy Research (Journal of Nephrology, April 2026)

A recent study developed and analyzed ADPKD-specific ketogenic diet meal plans, confirming that macronutrient targets can be met. However, it identified specific micronutrient gaps requiring supplementation, and highlighted unique nutritional constraints in ADPKD patients (potassium, phosphorus, sodium management alongside CKD dietary restrictions). This is useful practical guidance for dietitians but does not change the underlying evidence on whether ketogenic diets slow ADPKD progression.

### ⚠️ A Note on Commercial "Medical Food" Programs

Real-world data claiming a **6.3% eGFR improvement** with a commercially manufactured ketogenic medical food (Ren-Nu) was published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (January 2026). Patients may encounter this study online or through marketing materials. **Treat this claim with significant skepticism:**

1. The study was funded by the Ren-Nu manufacturer — a direct conflict of interest.
2. There was no control arm. In ADPKD, an apparent eGFR rise can result from stopping nephrotoxic medications, hemodynamic changes from diet, weight loss, or regression to mean — not the treatment itself.
3. A 6.3% eGFR *increase* in ADPKD is extraordinary. The typical course is progressive decline. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence — which this study does not provide.
4. Published in *Frontiers in Nutrition*, which has lower methodological standards than nephrology-specific journals.

The ketogenic dietary *approach* has genuine biological plausibility and is being tested in rigorous trials (KETO-ADPKD, GREASE II). The Ren-Nu study does not validate the approach — it only validates that the manufacturer can write a favorable real-world report. Patients curious about dietary interventions should wait for results from GREASE II or consider enrolling in a clinical trial.

**Another example, added August 2026:** A case series of just **4 patients** claimed to show "halting cyst progression" using long-term ketogenic metabolic therapy plus a commercial exogenous-ketone/alkaline-citrate product. This is an even weaker level of evidence than the Ren-Nu study above: four self-referred patients, no comparison group, each compared only to their own past growth trend, and the authors disclose direct financial ties to the product studied (company employment, shareholding, and patents). Do not treat this as evidence that any specific commercial ketone/citrate product slows or halts ADPKD progression.

### What a Systematic Review Says (March 2026)

A systematic review published in *Metabolism Open* (Grammatikopoulou et al., March 2026) synthesized 8 human studies of ketogenic and related dietary interventions in ADPKD. Key findings:

- **Metabolic effects:** KDIs generally produced weight loss and favorable anthropometric changes. Blood pressure and cholesterol were largely unchanged.
- **Kidney function (eGFR):** In 4 of the studies, eGFR was either stable or numerically higher after intervention. However, short follow-up times and no control arms in most studies limit interpretation.
- **Kidney volume (htTKV):** htTKV remained **unchanged** in most studies. Effects on renal structural outcomes were inconsistent.
- **Overall conclusion:** "Current human data support metabolic feasibility more consistently than renal disease modification." Longer trials with appropriate control arms are needed before KDIs can be recommended.

This review modestly lowers confidence in ketogenic diets for structural kidney benefit. The metabolic feasibility story remains intact. The structural (cyst-slowing) story requires better evidence.

### Obesity and Renal Blood Flow — cross-sectional association (Birznieks et al., March 2026)

A cross-sectional analysis of 134 non-diabetic ADPKD adults from the pravastatin RCT cohort (*Kidney and Blood Pressure Research*, Birznieks et al., March 2026) reported that BSA-adjusted renal blood flow (RBF) is lower in overweight and obese patients: after multivariable adjustment including eGFR and htTKV, overweight (beta −78.4) and obesity (beta −190.6) remained independently associated with lower RBF, and each 1-unit BMI increase was associated with a 10.4 mL/min/1.73m² lower RBF.

Paired with the earlier Nowak et al. finding that low RBF independently predicts long-term kidney failure (HR 3.19 for the lowest vs highest RBF tertile), this suggests that higher BMI may be one of several factors that reduce renal perfusion in ADPKD. The direction of causation is not established — advanced disease may also reduce activity and promote weight gain. The authors explicitly note "associations only."

**What this means for patients:** Weight management remains a reasonable general health goal, and there are now two independent observational signals (BMI→RBF and RBF→kidney failure) that are at least consistent with the idea that weight modification could plausibly help. But there is no interventional evidence yet that intentional weight loss changes ADPKD progression.

### Key Takeaways on Ketogenic Diets

- **Promising but not yet proven** as a standard recommendation for ADPKD. A March 2026 systematic review confirms metabolic feasibility but finds inconsistent evidence for kidney volume benefit.
- The degree of ketosis achieved appears to be the important factor, not the specific diet used to get there (from KETO-ADPKD post-hoc analyses).
- Safety in CKD stages 3-5 requires careful nutritional supervision, including micronutrient monitoring.
- Long-term sustainability is a real challenge -- many people find strict ketogenic diets difficult to maintain.
- Should not be attempted without medical supervision in ADPKD, especially if kidney function is already reduced.
- The 2025 KDIGO guideline does not yet recommend ketogenic diets as standard of care but acknowledges the emerging evidence.
- **Commercial products marketed to ADPKD patients should be treated skeptically** — company-sponsored real-world studies are not a substitute for controlled trials.

### ⚠️ Dietary KD vs Exogenous Ketone Supplementation — Not Interchangeable (APS 2026, May 2026)

An APS 2026 conference abstract (*Physiology*, May 2026, presented as "Ketogenic Communication in Polycystic Kidney Disease") reports the first comparative multi-omics (metabolomics + proteomics) study of ADPKD patients on a full **dietary** ketogenic diet versus a **ketogenic supplement diet (KSD)**. The key finding: **18 of 156 serum metabolites were consistently altered on KD, and dietary KD produced substantially superior metabolic reprogramming compared to KSD.** This means that exogenous ketone supplements (BHB drinks, ketone salts) do not replicate the full metabolic state achieved by dietary carbohydrate restriction.

**Why this matters:** The ongoing Godstrup Hospital BHB supplementation trial (NCT06867471) and the dietary KD trials (GREASE II, NCT06325644) are testing different biological interventions. A positive result in one would not validate the other.

**A cautionary signal:** The same abstract identified **taurine depletion** as a consistent metabolic consequence of the ketogenic diet. In a mouse experiment, combining taurine depletion with cysteamine (a compound being separately explored as a potential ADPKD therapeutic) **worsened cystic index**. This is a mouse-model mechanistic observation — not a human risk — but it identifies a potential drug-diet interaction that should be monitored prospectively. No ADPKD patients are currently being prescribed cysteamine specifically for ADPKD, so this signal is not currently actionable. However:
- If you are combining a ketogenic diet with any thiol-based supplements or compounds, discuss this with your nephrologist.
- Future ketogenic diet trials may need to monitor taurine levels as a safety biomarker.

**Limitations:** This is an abstract-only disclosure — no full methods, patient numbers, or statistical detail are publicly available. No TKV or eGFR endpoints were reported. The taurine finding is preclinical. This does not change the overall assessment of ketogenic diets as promising but unproven for structural kidney benefit.

## 2. Exogenous Ketone Supplementation (BHB)

If the benefits of ketogenic diets come from ketosis itself (rather than from the diet per se), then a logical alternative is to take ketone supplements and skip the dietary restriction altogether. This is the rationale behind beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) supplementation.

### Godstrup Hospital Trial (NCT06867471)

- **What:** A randomized, placebo-controlled trial at Godstrup Hospital (Denmark) testing R-1,3-butanediol (marketed as Ketone-IQ) versus placebo in 43 ADPKD patients.
- **Primary outcomes:** Effects on proteinuria (protein in the urine, a marker of kidney damage) and renal function.
- **Why it matters:** If exogenous ketones can replicate the kidney-protective effects seen with dietary ketosis, this would be a far more practical intervention. Most people find taking a supplement much easier than maintaining a strict ketogenic diet. This trial is the first rigorous test of that hypothesis in ADPKD patients.
- **Status as of April 2026:** Ongoing. This is a relatively new trial and results are not yet available.

### Practical Considerations

- BHB supplements are commercially available but not cheap.
- The dose needed to achieve meaningful blood ketone levels is not yet established for kidney protection purposes.
- Taste and gastrointestinal tolerance can be issues.
- Safety in patients with reduced kidney function is still being evaluated.

### ⚠️ "BHB: A Renoprotective Hormone" — A Review to Read Critically (Added August 2026)

A narrative review published in July 2026 by the Weimbs laboratory (UC Santa Barbara) makes the case that beta-hydroxybutyrate should be understood as a "renoprotective hormone" -- not just a fuel source but a signaling molecule with direct anti-cystic, anti-inflammatory, and mitochondria-supporting effects. It argues that modern diets chronically suppress the body's natural ketone production, depriving the kidneys of this protective signal.

**Why to read this critically:** This review contains no new experimental data -- it synthesizes existing preclinical and early clinical work. It is written by the same research group that runs the KETO-ADPKD and GREASE clinical trials referenced above, and this group has an established research and commercial interest in ketogenic and BHB-based ADPKD interventions. That does not make the underlying science wrong, but it means this particular document should be read as an advocacy synthesis from an invested group, not as independent confirmation. The title's framing of BHB as a proven "renoprotective hormone" goes further than what has actually been demonstrated in controlled human trials to date.

**Bottom line:** the metabolic rationale for ketogenic approaches in ADPKD remains genuinely interesting and worth discussing with your care team, but this review does not raise the evidence bar -- the same "promising but preliminary" verdict from the sections above still applies.

## 3. Caloric Restriction & Intermittent Fasting

Animal studies (mainly in mouse models of PKD) have shown strong effects of caloric restriction on slowing cyst growth. The mechanisms likely overlap with ketogenic diets -- both reduce glucose availability and activate cellular "housekeeping" pathways (autophagy) that may help prevent cyst formation.

### What We Know

- **Animal data is strong:** Multiple mouse studies show reduced cyst burden with 20-40% caloric restriction or time-restricted feeding.
- **Human data is limited:** The KETO-ADPKD trial (see above) included a water fasting arm (3-day fasts repeated monthly), which was feasible and associated with ketosis. However, the fasting arm was not powered to detect independent effects on kidney volume.
- **No dedicated human RCT for caloric restriction in ADPKD** has been completed as of April 2026.

### Practical Considerations

- Intermittent fasting (e.g., 16:8 time-restricted eating) is generally safe for most people but has not been specifically studied in ADPKD.
- Prolonged fasting (multi-day) carries risks including dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and muscle wasting, and should only be done under medical supervision.
- For patients on tolvaptan, fasting may complicate the already-challenging fluid management requirements.
- Caloric restriction should not lead to undernutrition, particularly in patients with advanced CKD who may already have protein-energy wasting.

## 4. Water Intake / Hydration Therapy

### The Rationale

Vasopressin (also called antidiuretic hormone, or ADH) is a key driver of cyst growth in ADPKD. Tolvaptan works by blocking the vasopressin receptor in the kidney. A non-pharmacological alternative is to suppress vasopressin naturally by drinking large amounts of water -- when the body is well-hydrated, it produces less vasopressin.

### Feasibility Study (Kidney360, April 2024)

- A study published in Kidney360 tested a structured water therapy protocol in ADPKD patients.
- **Results:** Increased water intake successfully reduced urine osmolality (a measure of urine concentration) and copeptin (a surrogate marker for vasopressin levels), confirming that the physiological mechanism works.
- **Limitation:** The study was a feasibility trial. It showed that the approach can lower vasopressin-related markers, but it did not demonstrate effects on kidney volume or disease progression.

### Practical Considerations

- The volume of water required for meaningful vasopressin suppression is substantial -- often 3 liters per day or more, spread throughout the day including before bed.
- This is challenging for many people and can interfere with sleep and daily activities.
- The approach is less potent than tolvaptan for vasopressin suppression.
- It is generally safe in patients with preserved kidney function (eGFR above 30), but excessive water intake can cause dangerously low sodium levels (hyponatremia) in patients with reduced kidney function.
- Patients already taking tolvaptan are instructed to drink large amounts of water to compensate for the drug's effects, so additional high-volume water intake on top of tolvaptan is generally not recommended without medical guidance.
- The KDIGO 2025 guideline acknowledges adequate hydration as a supportive measure but stops short of recommending prescriptive water intake targets.

## 5. Sodium Restriction

### The Evidence

- The HALT-PKD trials (landmark studies completed in 2014) demonstrated that strict blood pressure control combined with ACE inhibitor therapy slowed kidney growth. While the trials primarily tested blood pressure medications, all participants were counseled to limit sodium intake to less than 2,300 mg per day (about 1 teaspoon of salt).
- Observational analyses from HALT-PKD and other cohorts suggest that higher sodium intake is associated with faster kidney growth and higher blood pressure in ADPKD.
- The KDIGO 2025 guideline recommends sodium restriction (less than 2,300 mg/day, ideally less than 2,000 mg/day) for ADPKD patients, consistent with general CKD guidance.

### Practical Considerations

- Sodium restriction is one of the best-supported dietary recommendations for ADPKD.
- Most dietary sodium comes from processed and restaurant foods, not the salt shaker.
- Reading nutrition labels and cooking at home are the most effective strategies.
- Sodium restriction also helps blood pressure control, which is independently important in ADPKD.
- This is a relatively easy intervention to implement compared to ketogenic diets or high-volume water intake.

## 6. Protein Management

### Stage-Dependent Targets

- Protein management in ADPKD follows general CKD principles. The current recommendation is 0.8 g/kg body weight per day for CKD stages 3-5, with some guidelines suggesting 0.6-0.8 g/kg for advanced CKD to reduce the workload on remaining kidney function.
- In early-stage ADPKD with preserved kidney function, moderate protein intake (0.8-1.0 g/kg) is generally considered safe.

### Plant-Based vs. Animal Protein

- There is growing interest in whether the *source* of protein matters. Some observational studies in CKD suggest that plant-based protein may be less harmful to kidneys than animal protein, possibly because plant proteins generate fewer uremic toxins and less acid load.
- No ADPKD-specific RCT has tested plant-based versus animal protein diets as of April 2026.
- A plant-forward diet is consistent with general kidney health recommendations and carries minimal risk.

### Practical Considerations

- Excessive protein restriction can lead to muscle wasting, especially in older patients or those with advanced CKD.
- Protein needs may increase during acute illness or after surgery.
- A registered dietitian with kidney expertise is the best resource for individualized protein targets.

## 7. Gut Microbiome

This is an emerging area with limited but intriguing data.

### What Is Known

- The gut microbiome (the community of bacteria in the intestines) produces metabolites that can either protect or harm the kidneys. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), produced when gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber, have anti-inflammatory properties and may protect kidney function.
- Conversely, certain gut-derived toxins (such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate) are elevated in CKD and may accelerate kidney damage.
- A few preclinical studies suggest that the gut microbiome is altered in PKD and that modifying it (through diet, prebiotics, or probiotics) might influence cyst growth.

### Practical Considerations

- A high-fiber diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains supports a healthy gut microbiome and is consistent with general kidney health recommendations.
- There are no ADPKD-specific probiotic or microbiome-targeted interventions proven effective as of April 2026.
- Patients with advanced CKD may need to limit certain high-fiber foods due to potassium or phosphorus content, which complicates blanket fiber recommendations.
- This is an area to watch, but not one where specific action is warranted beyond general healthy eating.

## 8. Weight Management

### BMI and ADPKD Progression

- Higher body mass index (BMI) has been associated with faster kidney growth and worse outcomes in several ADPKD cohorts.
- Obesity is also a risk factor for hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease -- all of which compound kidney disease.
- Maintaining a healthy weight is one of the most impactful general health measures for ADPKD patients.

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

- GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide/Ozempic, tirzepatide/Mounjaro) are transforming obesity treatment. These drugs cause substantial weight loss and have shown kidney-protective effects in type 2 diabetes.
- The first dedicated ADPKD GLP-1 RA trial — **NCT06582875** — is now recruiting. It tests tirzepatide specifically in **obese ADPKD patients** (elevated BMI or significant metabolic dysfunction as an inclusion criterion). This patient-selection strategy is notable in light of new preclinical data (see below).
- Some ADPKD patients are already prescribed GLP-1 RAs for weight management or diabetes; any kidney-related effects observed in this population will be informative.

#### ⚠️ Preclinical Caveat: Anti-Cystic Effect May Be Conditional on Obesity (APS 2026, May 2026)

An APS 2026 abstract tested semaglutide in a preclinical ADPKD mouse model under two conditions: **high-fat diet (HFD)** and **standard chow**. The key finding: semaglutide slowed cyst progression in HFD mice but showed **no detectable anti-cystic effect in chow-fed, metabolically normal mice**.

This diet-dependent response suggests that the anti-cystic mechanism of GLP-1 RAs may be **metabolic rescue** (reversing obesity-driven cyst acceleration) rather than a direct renal effect independent of metabolic state.

**Implications:**
- GLP-1 RA benefit in ADPKD may be restricted to overweight or obese patients. Lean ADPKD patients may see little to no renal benefit.
- The tirzepatide trial design (targeting obese patients) is appropriate given this signal. Subgroup pre-specification by baseline BMI would strengthen future trial interpretability.
- This does **not** invalidate the trial rationale — high-fat diet models in PKD are established models of metabolic-driven disease acceleration, and "metabolic rescue" is a plausible and clinically meaningful mechanism.
- The finding also contextualizes the BMI→RBF association (see §1 above): weight-related pathophysiology may be an especially important ADPKD modifier.

**Limitations:** Abstract-only disclosure; no full methods, sample sizes, or statistical detail. Mouse model results in ADPKD have historically poor translational fidelity. Human validation required before drawing firm conclusions.

*Added May 31, 2026 — APS 2026 semaglutide preclinical abstract incorporated.*

**Added August 2026:** A translational review article pulled together the existing rationale for GLP-1 receptor agonists in ADPKD (including the semaglutide mouse data above) and the design logic behind the tirzepatide trial. It presents no new experimental data, and the reviewing authors themselves note that the case for a real therapeutic effect remains incomplete. This is useful background reading, not new evidence — treat it as a pipeline-context item, not a reason to update expectations.

### Practical Considerations

- Weight management through diet and exercise is safe and recommended for ADPKD patients.
- Crash diets and rapid weight loss should be avoided, as they can cause electrolyte imbalances and muscle wasting.
- Exercise is safe and beneficial -- there is no evidence that physical activity accelerates cyst growth.

## 9. Comprehensive Reviews

Two major review articles provide useful overviews of the dietary landscape in ADPKD:

### "PKD Diet: What Is Known and What Is Safe" (CJASN, September 2023)

Published in the Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, this review synthesized the evidence for various dietary interventions in ADPKD. It covers ketogenic diets, caloric restriction, hydration, sodium, and protein. The key message is that while several dietary approaches show promise in animal models, human evidence is still limited. The review emphasizes safety considerations, particularly the risk of combining restrictive diets with the dietary limitations already imposed by CKD.

### "Diet and Physical Activity in Adult Dominant PKD" (Nutrients, June 2023)

This review in the journal Nutrients provides a broader look at both dietary and physical activity interventions. It includes a helpful framework for thinking about which interventions have strong versus weak evidence, and which are low-risk enough to consider even without definitive proof of benefit.

## Summary: What You Can Do Now

The following table summarizes each intervention by the strength of evidence for ADPKD-specific benefit and the general safety profile.

| Intervention | Evidence Level | Safety | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium restriction (<2,300 mg/day) | Strong (HALT-PKD, KDIGO 2025) | Safe | Recommended for all ADPKD patients |
| Adequate hydration | Moderate (physiological rationale, feasibility data) | Safe with normal kidney function | Reasonable, discuss targets with your doctor |
| Healthy weight maintenance | Strong (general CKD data, observational ADPKD data) | Safe | Recommended |
| Moderate protein intake (0.8 g/kg) | Moderate (CKD guidelines) | Safe | Follow CKD-stage-appropriate targets |
| Plant-forward diet | Weak-to-moderate (observational CKD data) | Safe | Reasonable, low-risk |
| High-fiber diet | Weak (emerging microbiome data) | Generally safe | Reasonable, consistent with overall health |
| Ketogenic diet | Promising (KETO-ADPKD, ongoing trials) | Requires medical supervision | Do not attempt without a healthcare team |
| Exogenous ketone supplements | Early (trial ongoing) | Unknown in ADPKD | Wait for trial results |
| Caloric restriction / fasting | Strong in animals, limited in humans | Requires medical supervision | Not recommended without guidance |

## What to Watch

- **GREASE II results** -- the 24-month ketogenic diet trial will be the most important dietary data point in the near term.
- **Godstrup BHB trial (NCT06867471)** -- if exogenous ketones work, this could change the dietary landscape dramatically.
- **Ohio State WFKD study (NCT06325644)** -- will provide data on a structured, supervised ketogenic approach.
- **Tirzepatide trial (NCT06582875)** — first dedicated GLP-1 RA RCT in ADPKD (obese patients); a positive result would confirm the metabolic conditionality hypothesis from preclinical data.
- **KDIGO guideline updates** -- future revisions may incorporate stronger dietary recommendations as trial data accumulates.
